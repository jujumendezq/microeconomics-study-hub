📄 Exam 1: Methodology & The Market

Time: 60 minutes | Total Points: 100

Covering chapters 1-2: Methodology and Supply & Demand

1. Define economics and explain the difference between microeconomics and macroeconomics. (10 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

Economics is the study of how individuals, firms, and societies make choices when faced with scarcity. It examines how people allocate limited resources to satisfy unlimited wants.

Microeconomics studies individual agents (consumers, firms) and specific markets. It focuses on decisions at the individual level.
Example: How does a firm decide how many workers to hire?

Macroeconomics looks at the economy as a whole using aggregate measures like GDP, inflation, and unemployment.
Example: Why is inflation rising?

Key distinction: Micro = individual trees; Macro = whole forest.
2. Explain the difference between positive and normative analysis, and provide an example of each. (10 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

Positive Analysis is about "what IS" — it describes how the world works and makes testable claims. It is objective and based on facts.
Example: "A 10% increase in the minimum wage would reduce employment by 2% among low-skilled workers."

Normative Analysis is about "what SHOULD BE" — it makes value judgments and recommendations based on opinions and beliefs. It is subjective.
Example: "We should not raise the minimum wage because it hurts workers."

Key distinction: Positive = factual and testable; Normative = value-based and opinionated.
3. Given QD = 150 − 6P and QS = 30 + 4P, find the equilibrium price and quantity. (15 points)
✅ Solution:

Set QD = QS:
150 − 6P = 30 + 4P
150 − 30 = 4P + 6P
120 = 10P
P* = 12

Q* = 150 − 6(12) = 150 − 72 = 78

Answer: Equilibrium price is $12, equilibrium quantity is 78 units.
4. Explain the concept of opportunity cost and provide a real-world example. (10 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

Opportunity Cost is the value of the best alternative that you give up when you make a choice. It's the true cost of any decision—not just the money you spend, but everything you sacrifice.

Example: Maria gives up a $50,000/year job to start a business. Her accounting costs are $80,000/year and revenue is $120,000/year.
Accounting profit = $120,000 − $80,000 = $40,000
Opportunity cost = $50,000 (forgone salary)
Economic profit = $40,000 − $50,000 = −$10,000

Conclusion: The business LOOKS profitable ($40,000 accounting profit) but is actually a bad decision (−$10,000 economic loss). The true cost includes the forgone salary.
5. Coffee and tea are substitutes. If the price of coffee increases, what happens to the demand for tea? Explain. (10 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

When the price of a substitute (coffee) increases, consumers switch to the alternative (tea), causing demand for tea to increase.

Effect: The entire demand curve for tea shifts to the RIGHT.

Result: Higher equilibrium price and higher equilibrium quantity for tea.

Reason: This is a shift of the demand curve, NOT a movement along it, because the change is due to the price of a related good (coffee), not the price of tea itself.
6. A technological breakthrough reduces production costs for smartphones. Analyze the effect on equilibrium price and quantity. (10 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

A technological breakthrough shifts the supply curve to the RIGHT (increase in supply).

Effect on equilibrium:
• Price: DECREASES
• Quantity: INCREASES

Reason: At any given price, firms can now produce more smartphones. This puts downward pressure on price and increases the quantity traded.

📄 Exam 2: Elasticity & Welfare

Time: 60 minutes | Total Points: 100

Covering chapters 3-4: Elasticity, Consumer Surplus, and Producer Surplus

1. The price of a good increases from $10 to $12, and quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80. Calculate the own-price elasticity of demand. (10 points)
✅ Solution:

%ΔQ = (80 − 100) / 100 = −20%
%ΔP = (12 − 10) / 10 = 20%
ε = %ΔQ / %ΔP = −20% / 20% = −1.0

|ε| = 1 → Demand is unit-elastic.

Interpretation: A 1% increase in price leads to exactly a 1% decrease in quantity demanded.
2. If demand is elastic (|ε| > 1), what happens to total revenue when price increases? Explain. (10 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

When demand is elastic (|ε| > 1), the quantity effect dominates the price effect.

• Price effect: Higher price increases revenue on each unit sold (pushes TR up)
• Quantity effect: Fewer units are sold (pushes TR down)

Since demand is elastic, the percentage decrease in quantity is larger than the percentage increase in price.

Conclusion: Total revenue DECREASES when price increases.
3. The price of Coke increases by 10%, and demand for Pepsi increases by 15%. Calculate the cross-price elasticity. Are they substitutes or complements? (10 points)
✅ Solution:

ε = %ΔQPepsi / %ΔPCoke = 15% / 10% = 1.5

Interpretation:
• Positive elasticity → Substitutes
• A 1% increase in Coke price leads to a 1.5% increase in Pepsi demand

Answer: Coke and Pepsi are substitutes with cross-price elasticity of 1.5.
4. Given QD = 100 − 2P and QS = 20 + 2P, find equilibrium price, quantity, consumer surplus, and producer surplus. (20 points)
✅ Solution:

Step 1: Find equilibrium
100 − 2P = 20 + 2P → 80 = 4P → P* = 20
Q* = 100 − 2(20) = 60

Step 2: Find intercepts
Demand intercept: Q=0 → P = 50
Supply intercept: Q=0 → P = −10 (but the minimum price is where Q=0, so P_min = -10, though for surplus we use the intercept where supply meets price axis, which is -10)

Step 3: Calculate CS
CS = ½ × Q* × (Pmax − P*) = ½ × 60 × (50 − 20) = ½ × 60 × 30 = 900

Step 4: Calculate PS
PS = ½ × Q* × (P* − Pmin) = ½ × 60 × (20 − (−10)) = ½ × 60 × 30 = 900

Answer: P* = 20, Q* = 60, CS = 900, PS = 900

📄 Exam 3: Government Interventions & Monopoly

Time: 60 minutes | Total Points: 100

Covering chapters 5, 7, and 8: Government Interventions, Perfect Competition, and Monopoly

1. A tax is imposed on a good. Buyers pay $12 and sellers receive $8. What is the tax per unit? (5 points)
✅ Solution:

Tax = PD − PS = $12 − $8 = $4
2. A monopolist has TC = 16 + Q² and faces demand Q = 24 − P. Find the monopoly equilibrium price, quantity, profit, and deadweight loss. (25 points)
✅ Solution:

Step 1: Find MR
P = 24 − Q
TR = P × Q = 24Q − Q²
MR = 24 − 2Q

Step 2: Find MC
MC = dTC/dQ = 2Q

Step 3: Set MR = MC
24 − 2Q = 2Q → 4Q = 24 → QM = 6
PM = 24 − 6 = 18

Step 4: Calculate Profit
TR = 18 × 6 = 108
TC = 16 + 36 = 52
π = 108 − 52 = 56

Step 5: Calculate DWL
Competitive Q: Set P = MC → 24 − Q = 2Q → QPC = 8
DWL = ½ × (PM − PPC) × (QPC − QM)
PPC = 2(8) = 16
DWL = ½ × (18 − 16) × (8 − 6) = ½ × 2 × 2 = 2

Answer: QM = 6, PM = 18, π = 56, DWL = 2
3. A firm has TC = 500 + 0.25Q² and faces P = 50 in a perfectly competitive market. Find the profit-maximizing quantity and profit. (15 points)
✅ Solution:

Step 1: Find MC
MC = dTC/dQ = 0.5Q

Step 2: Set P = MC
50 = 0.5Q → Q = 100

Step 3: Calculate Profit
TR = 50 × 100 = 5000
TC = 500 + 0.25(10000) = 500 + 2500 = 3000
π = 5000 − 3000 = 2000

Answer: Q = 100, π = 2000
4. A price ceiling is set below equilibrium. What happens to quantity traded? Which quantity determines the trade? (10 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

When a price ceiling is set below equilibrium:

• Quantity demanded increases (more people want the good at the lower price)
• Quantity supplied decreases (fewer sellers willing to supply at the lower price)
• Result: A shortage (excess demand)

Quantity traded = QS (the quantity supplied)

Why? The short side of the market prevails. You can't force sellers to supply more than they're willing to at the controlled price.

📄 Exam 4: Oligopoly & Game Theory

Time: 60 minutes | Total Points: 100

Covering chapter 9: Oligopoly, Game Theory & Externalities

1. Two firms in a duopoly have P = 13 − (Q₁ + Q₂) and MC = 1. Find the non-cooperative (Cournot-Nash) equilibrium quantity for each firm. (15 points)
✅ Solution:

Step 1: Set up profit functions
π₁ = (13 − Q₁ − Q₂ − 1) × Q₁ = (12 − Q₁ − Q₂) × Q₁
π₂ = (13 − Q₂ − Q₁ − 1) × Q₂ = (12 − Q₂ − Q₁) × Q₂

Step 2: Find best response functions
FOC for Firm 1: 12 − 2Q₁ − Q₂ = 0 → Q₁ = (12 − Q₂)/2
FOC for Firm 2: 12 − 2Q₂ − Q₁ = 0 → Q₂ = (12 − Q₁)/2

Step 3: Solve simultaneously
Q₁ = (12 − (12 − Q₁)/2)/2 → Q₁ = 4, Q₂ = 4

Answer: Q₁ = 4, Q₂ = 4
2. In the Prisoner's Dilemma, what is the Nash equilibrium and why is it not Pareto optimal? (10 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

In the Prisoner's Dilemma:

Nash Equilibrium: (Confess, Confess)

Why it's NOT Pareto optimal:
• Both prisoners would be better off with (Not Confess, Not Confess) → 1 year each
• But (Not Confess, Not Confess) is NOT a Nash equilibrium
• Each prisoner has an incentive to cheat (confess) to get 0 years
• The Nash equilibrium (Confess, Confess) gives 8 years each

Key insight: Pareto optimal means no one can be made better off without making someone worse off. (Not Confess, Not Confess) is Pareto superior to (Confess, Confess), but it's not stable.
3. Define externalities and explain why they represent a market failure. Provide one example of a negative externality and one example of a positive externality. (15 points)
✅ Sample Answer:

Externality: An uncompensated impact of one person's actions on another person's well-being.

Why externalities are market failures:
• The market price does not reflect the full social cost or benefit
• Negative externality → overproduction (too much of the good)
• Positive externality → underproduction (too little of the good)

Example of Negative Externality:
Factory pollution. The factory produces goods but doesn't pay for the health and environmental costs imposed on the community.

Example of Positive Externality:
Education. When you get an education, you benefit from higher earnings (private benefit), but society also benefits from a more productive workforce and higher tax revenues (social benefit).
