🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to Microeconomics! This first seminar introduces you to the fundamental building blocks of all economic thinking. Think of microeconomics as the study of how individuals and firms make decisions when faced with scarce resources. Every choice you make—whether to study or go out with friends, whether to buy coffee or tea, whether to work or pursue a degree—involves trade-offs. This seminar teaches you how to think like an economist by understanding opportunity costs, analyzing markets, and distinguishing between correlation and causation.

Why should you care? Because these concepts aren't just academic—they explain everything from why roses are expensive on Valentine's Day to why traffic jams happen. By the end of this seminar, you'll be able to analyze real-world decisions and market outcomes with an economist's toolkit.

🔹 Concept 1: Opportunity Cost

Definition: Opportunity Cost is the value of the best alternative you give up when making a choice between two or more options.

Think of it as the "road not taken"—whenever you choose one thing, you're saying "no" to everything else you could have done with that time, money, or resource. The opportunity cost is the value of the best of those alternatives you gave up.

Real-World Example:

You have $20 and one hour. You can either:

  • Option A: Go to the movies ($15 ticket + 2 hours of your time)
  • Option B: Work a part-time job ($20/hour for 2 hours = $40)

The opportunity cost of going to the movies isn't just the $15 ticket—it's the $40 you could have earned working PLUS the $15 you spent. The full cost of the movie is $55!

Why do we care about opportunity cost? Because money isn't the only cost. Every decision uses scarce resources:

  • Time is scarce (24 hours a day)
  • Money is scarce (limited budget)
  • Energy is scarce (can't do everything)

If you only consider money, you miss the bigger picture. The true cost of any decision includes what you sacrifice to make that choice.

ElementExplanation
Not Just MonetaryOpportunity cost includes non-money costs like time, enjoyment, and convenience

🔹 Concept 2: Microeconomics vs. Macroeconomics

Microeconomics studies the choices of individual economic agents—households, firms, and individual markets.

Macroeconomics studies the behavior of the entire economy as a whole—national income, inflation, unemployment, and economic growth.

Think of the economy like a forest:
  • Microeconomics = studying individual trees (how one tree grows, how much sunlight it needs, how it competes with nearby trees)
  • Macroeconomics = studying the whole forest (how many trees total, the overall health of the forest, the climate affecting all trees)
Microeconomics FocusMacroeconomics Focus
Individual household consumption/savingNational consumption and total investment
Firm pricing and staffing decisionsBanking system and government taxation
Single market for specific goodsNational savings and economic growth
Individual optimizationInflation and money supply

🔹 Concept 3: Correlation vs. Causality

Correlation: Two variables move together (when X goes up, Y goes up or down).

Causality: A change in X directly causes a change in Y (X → Y).

⚠️ The Key Insight: Correlation does NOT imply causation! Just because two things happen together doesn't mean one causes the other.

Three Types of Correlation/Causality Problems:

TypeDefinitionExample
Reverse CausalityY causes X, not X causes YHospital beds don't kill you; being sick (Y) puts you in a hospital bed (X)
Spurious CorrelationZ causes both X and YIce cream sales and violent crime both increase in warm weather (Z)
Omitted Variable BiasAn unobserved variable affects both X and YHealthier people (Z) are more likely to marry AND live longer
Real-World Example: Teacher Salaries and Test Results
  • Observation: Pupils do better in smaller classes with teachers paid more.
  • Correct conclusion: There exists a correlation between teacher salaries and test results.
  • Wrong conclusion: This proves that teacher salaries influence student performance.
  • Why? There might be omitted variables (wealthier schools have both smaller classes AND better test results).

🔹 Concept 4: Market Equilibrium

Definition: Market Equilibrium is the point where the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded. At this point, the market "clears"—everyone who wants to buy at the equilibrium price can, and everyone who wants to sell at that price can.

At equilibrium: QD = QS
ConceptDefinition
DemandQuantity buyers want to purchase at each price (downward sloping)
SupplyQuantity sellers want to sell at each price (upward sloping)
Equilibrium Price (P*)Price where QD = QS
Equilibrium Quantity (Q*)Quantity where QD = QS
Excess Demand (Shortage)QD > QS (price is below equilibrium)
Excess Supply (Surplus)QS > QD (price is above equilibrium)
Real-World Examples of Excess Demand:
  • Traffic jams: The "good" is road space. Supply is fixed, but demand exceeds supply during peak times.
  • Waiting lists for rental apartments: More people want apartments than are available at the current price.
  • Black market for concert tickets: Tickets sell at prices above face value, indicating excess demand at the official price.

🧮 Key Formulas

Equilibrium: QS = QD
Economic Cost = Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs
Economic Rent = Benefit − Economic Cost
Decision Rule: If Benefit > Economic Cost → Take the action

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Opportunity Cost – Concert Choice

Scenario: Concert A in a theater: $25 admission. Concert B in a park: FREE (but happens at the same time). You would have been willing to pay $15 to attend Concert B if it was NOT free.

Solution:

  • Benefit of next best alternative to Concert A: $15 (opportunity cost)
  • Total economic cost of Concert A: $25 + $15 = $40
  • If you anticipate $35 worth of pleasure from Concert A → Don't go ($35 < $40)
  • If you anticipate $50 worth of pleasure from Concert A → Go ($50 > $40)

Scenario 2: Correlation vs. Causation – Teacher Salaries

Scenario: On average, pupils do better at school if they study in smaller classes and are taught by teachers that are paid more.

Solution:

  • Correct: There exists a positive correlation between teacher salaries and test results, and a negative correlation between class size and test results.
  • Incorrect: The observation proves that teacher salaries and class size influence student performance.
  • Why? We can't prove causality—there might be omitted variables (wealthier schools have both).

Scenario 3: Excess Demand

Scenario: Which of the following are cases of excess demand?

Solution: Traffic jams, waiting lists for rental apartments, black market for concert tickets are cases of excess demand. Minimum wage unemployment is excess supply of labor.

Scenario 4: Demand and Supply Shifts – Wheat and Corn

Scenario: Heavy rains cause a negative supply shock to wheat, and the price of corn (a substitute for wheat) increases. What happens to the wheat market?

Solution:

  • Supply shock: Wheat supply shifts LEFT → Price ↑, Quantity ↓
  • Substitute effect: Corn price ↑ → Demand for wheat shifts RIGHT → Price ↑, Quantity ↑
  • Net effect: Price increases (certain); Quantity is undetermined (depends on which effect is larger)
Answer: The price of wheat increases but the effect on the quantity exchanged is undetermined.

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing "Demand" with "Quantity Demanded": Price changes cause movements along the curve; other factors cause shifts.
  • Assuming Correlation = Causation: Always look for third factors or reverse causality.
  • Forgetting Opportunity Cost: The true cost includes what you gave up.
  • Confusing Substitutes and Complements: If coffee price increases → demand for tea (substitute) INCREASES.

The "If This, Then That" Logic

If...Then...
Change in the good's OWN priceMovement ALONG the curve
Change in ANYTHING ELSEShift of the ENTIRE curve
Price is below equilibriumExcess demand (shortage)
Price is above equilibriumExcess supply (surplus)
Two variables move togetherCorrelation (NOT causation)

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: The opportunity cost of going to college includes:
✅ Correct Answer: C
Opportunity cost includes BOTH explicit costs (tuition, books) AND implicit costs (the income you could have earned working).
Q2: Which of the following is a microeconomics question?
✅ Correct Answer: A
Microeconomics focuses on individual agents and specific markets. Avocado prices are a specific market question. B, C, and D are macroeconomics questions.
Q3: A study finds that people who own pets live longer. A journalist concludes: "Owning a pet extends your lifespan." This is an example of:
✅ Correct Answer: B
The journalist is confusing correlation with causation. Healthier people may be more able to care for pets (reverse causality), or wealthier people may afford both pets and healthcare (third variable).
Q4: Given QD = 5 − P and QS = P − 1, what is the equilibrium price and quantity?
✅ Correct Answer: B
Set QD = QS: 5 − P = P − 1 → 6 = 2P → P* = 3 → Q* = 5 − 3 = 2
Q5: Coffee and tea are substitutes. If the price of coffee increases, what happens to the demand for tea?
✅ Correct Answer: B
When the price of a substitute increases, consumers switch to the alternative, causing demand for the other good to increase (shift RIGHT).

Macroeconomics

Correlation
A statistical relationship where two variables move together.
Causality
A relationship where one variable directly causes a change in another.

When Y causes X, rather than X causing Y.

When quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
Excess Supply (Surplus)
When quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.

Goods used in place of each other.
Complements
Goods used together.
Economic Cost
The sum of explicit and implicit costs.
Economic Rent
The difference between benefit and economic cost.

A direct monetary cost.
Implicit Cost
