🧑‍🏫 Seminar 3: Market Equilibrium, Government Interventions & Cost Functions

Taxes, subsidies, price controls, and production costs

🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to Seminar 3! This session bridges two critical areas of microeconomics: how markets work with government intervention (taxes, subsidies, price controls) and how firms think about their production costs. Why should you care? Because governments constantly intervene in markets—from rent control in New York to minimum wage laws in Switzerland—and understanding who really bears the burden of these policies is essential for any economist or informed citizen. Additionally, understanding cost structures is the foundation for analyzing how firms make production decisions, which we'll build on throughout the course.

🔹 Consumer Surplus

Definition: Consumer surplus (CS) is the difference between what consumers are willing to pay for a good and what they actually pay. It measures the extra benefit consumers receive from buying at market price.

Real-World Example: You're willing to pay up to 50 CHF for a concert ticket, but you find one for 30 CHF. Your consumer surplus is 20 CHF—the "savings" you enjoy from paying less than your maximum willingness to pay.
CS = Willingness to Pay − Market Price
For linear demand: CS = ½ × (Pmax − P*) × Q*

🔹 Producer Surplus

Definition: Producer surplus (PS) is the difference between what producers receive for a good and the minimum price they would be willing to accept to produce it.

Real-World Example: A farmer is willing to sell grapes for at least 2.50 CHF per kg (her minimum acceptable price), but the market price is 6 CHF per kg. Her producer surplus is 3.50 CHF per kg.
PS = Market Price − Willingness to Sell
For linear supply: PS = ½ × (P* − Pmin) × Q*

🔹 Tax Incidence

Definition: Tax incidence refers to how the burden of a tax is distributed between consumers and producers. It does NOT depend on who legally pays the tax—it depends on the relative elasticities of supply and demand.

⚠️ The Golden Rule: The more inelastic (steeper) a curve is, the more burden that side bears. If demand is inelastic, consumers can't easily reduce consumption and absorb more of the tax. If supply is inelastic, producers absorb more.
Tax = PD − PS
Tax Revenue = Tax × Qt
DWL = ½ × Tax × (Q* − Qt)
Example from Exercise 1:
  • Demand: QD = 100 − 2P
  • Supply: QS = −20 + 8P
  • Tax = 10
  • Initial: P* = 12, Q* = 76
  • After tax: Qt = 60, PD = 20, PS = 10
  • Consumers pay 8 more; producers receive 2 less
  • Tax Revenue = 10 × 60 = 600
  • DWL = ½ × 10 × (76 − 60) = 80

🔹 Subsidies

Definition: A subsidy is a government payment to producers (or consumers) that lowers production costs or purchase prices. It is the opposite of a tax.

Subsidy = PS − PD
Government Expenditure = Subsidy × Qsubsidy
Example from Exercise 3:
  • Demand: QD = 100 − 5P
  • Supply: QS = −50 + 20P
  • Target consumer price: PD = 3
  • Q = 100 − 5(3) = 85
  • PS = (85 + 50)/20 = 6.75
  • Subsidy = 6.75 − 3 = 3.75
  • Government Expenditure = 3.75 × 85 = 318.75
⚠️ Key Insight: Subsidies make both consumers and producers better off, but the government must pay for them. The deadweight loss is the "waste"—the amount the government spends that doesn't translate into benefits for either group.

🔹 Price Ceilings

Definition: A price ceiling is a government-imposed maximum price that sellers can charge for a good or service. It only has an effect if set below the equilibrium price.

Real-World Example: Rent control in New York City. The government sets a maximum rent of 1,000 USD when the equilibrium rent would be 1,600 USD.
⚠️ Key Effects:
  • Quantity demanded increases (more people want the cheap good)
  • Quantity supplied decreases (fewer sellers willing to supply at the low price)
  • The result is a shortage (excess demand)
  • Quantity exchanged = QS (the smaller quantity)

🔹 Price Floors (Minimum Wage)

Definition: A price floor is a government-imposed minimum price that sellers can charge. It only has an effect if set above the equilibrium price.

Real-World Example: The minimum wage is a price floor on labor—a government-mandated minimum price that employers must pay workers.
⚠️ Key Effects:
  • Quantity supplied increases (more workers want to work)
  • Quantity demanded decreases (fewer employers want to hire)
  • The result is a surplus (excess supply)—in labor markets, this is unemployment
  • Quantity exchanged = QD (the smaller quantity)
The Monopsony Counter-Argument:
  • In perfectly competitive labor markets, minimum wages create unemployment
  • But in monopsony markets (employers have market power), minimum wages can actually increase employment!
  • Card & Krueger (1994): Studied New Jersey's minimum wage increase → found no negative effect on employment
  • Card won the 2021 Nobel Prize for this work

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing "change in demand" with "change in quantity demanded": Tax affects quantity (movement along the curve), not demand (shift).
  • Thinking tax burden is always split 50/50: Burden depends on relative elasticities.
  • Confusing price ceilings and price floors: Ceiling → SHORTAGE; Floor → SURPLUS.
  • Thinking subsidies eliminate deadweight loss: Subsidies increase CS and PS, but still create DWL.
  • Confusing average cost with marginal cost: AC is average cost per unit; MC is cost of the LAST unit.
  • Forgetting to divide by the change in quantity: MC = ΔTC/Δq (if q changes by more than 1, divide).

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: A price ceiling set below equilibrium creates:
✅ Correct Answer: B
A price ceiling set below equilibrium creates a shortage (excess demand) because quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
Q2: A price floor set above equilibrium creates:
✅ Correct Answer: B
A price floor set above equilibrium creates a surplus (excess supply) because quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.
Q3: In a shortage, the quantity traded is determined by:
✅ Correct Answer: B
In a shortage, the short side of the market prevails. Since sellers control how much they supply, the quantity traded is QS.
Q4: A tax is imposed on a good. Buyers pay $12 and sellers receive $8. What is the tax per unit?
✅ Correct Answer: A
Tax = PD − PS = $12 − $8 = $4
Q5: The more inelastic side of the market bears:
✅ Correct Answer: B
The more inelastic side bears more of the tax burden because they can't easily adjust their behavior.

📖 Glossary

Consumer Surplus
Difference between willingness to pay and actual price paid.
Producer Surplus
Difference between price received and minimum willingness to sell.
Tax Incidence
How the burden of a tax is shared between buyers and sellers.
Deadweight Loss
The loss of economic efficiency from market interventions.
Price Ceiling
A legal maximum price (creates shortage if below equilibrium).
Price Floor
A legal minimum price (creates surplus if above equilibrium).
Shortage
Excess demand (QD > QS).
Surplus
Excess supply (QS > QD).
Fixed Cost
A cost that does not vary with output.
Variable Cost
A cost that varies with output.
Total Cost
Sum of fixed and variable costs.
Average Cost
Total cost divided by quantity.
Average Variable Cost
Variable cost divided by quantity.
Marginal Cost
The cost of producing one additional unit.
Marginal Product
The additional output from hiring one more worker.
