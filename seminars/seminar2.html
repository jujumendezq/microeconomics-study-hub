Measuring responsiveness and economic welfare

  • Distinguish between normal goods, inferior goods, substitutes, and complements using elasticity signs

🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to your second microeconomics seminar! This session is all about elasticity—which is just a fancy word for responsiveness. Think of it as measuring how sensitive people are to changes in prices, income, or other factors. Why does this matter? Because businesses need to know if raising prices will make customers flee (elastic) or stick around (inelastic). Governments need to know if taxing cigarettes will actually reduce smoking or just make smokers poorer. Understanding elasticity helps you predict human behavior in response to economic changes. We'll also explore market surplus—the total benefit that buyers and sellers get from participating in a market.

Key Insight: Elasticity is about percentages, not absolute numbers. This makes it super useful because we can compare completely different goods. A 1% price change on a $1 candy bar is just 1 cent, but a 1% price change on a $30,000 car is $300! Percentages let us compare apples to oranges (literally!).

🔹 What IS Elasticity?

Definition: Elasticity measures how much one variable responds to a change in another variable. In economics, we're usually asking: "If I change X by 1%, by what percentage does Y change?"

Real-World Examples:
  • If Starbucks raises coffee prices by 10%, how many fewer cups will people buy?
  • If the economy booms and everyone's income rises by 5%, how much more sushi will people order?
  • If the price of iPhones drops, how many more phone cases will sell?
Elasticity = %Δ Variable A / %Δ Variable B

🔹 Own-Price Elasticity of Demand

Definition: The own-price elasticity of demand measures the percentage change in quantity demanded of a good in response to a 1% change in the price of that same good.

εd = %ΔQd / %ΔP = (ΔQd/Qd) / (ΔP/P)

Three Categories (Using Absolute Value)

Category|εd|MeaningReal-World Example
Elastic> 11% price change causes >1% quantity changeLuxury cars, restaurant meals
Unit Elastic= 11% price change causes exactly 1% quantity changeThe "midpoint" of a linear demand curve
Inelastic< 11% price change causes <1% quantity changeInsulin, gasoline, basic food
⚠️ Exam tip: Own-price elasticity is almost always negative because of the Law of Demand. Economists often talk about the absolute value |εd| to avoid the negative sign.

Step-by-Step Example

Scenario: Swiss Airlines offers flights from Barcelona to Geneva. Original price: 500 CHF → 10 passengers. Christmas special: 300 CHF → 20 passengers. Calculate price elasticity of demand.

  • P₁ = 500, Q₁ = 10; P₂ = 300, Q₂ = 20
  • %ΔQ = (20 − 10)/10 = 100%
  • %ΔP = (300 − 500)/500 = −40%
  • ε = 100% / −40% = −2.5
  • |ε| = 2.5 → Elastic
⚠️ Common Mistake: Always divide by the initial value, not the new value! WRONG: (500−300)/300 = 200/300. CORRECT: (300−500)/500 = −200/500.

🔹 Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand

Definition: The cross-price elasticity of demand measures the percentage change in quantity demanded of Good X in response to a 1% change in the price of Good Y.

εpyx = %ΔQx / %ΔPy
SignThe goods are...Example
ε > 0SubstitutesIf coffee gets expensive, people buy more tea
ε < 0ComplementsIf phones get expensive, people buy fewer phone cases
ε ≈ 0UnrelatedComputers and grain
⚠️ IMPORTANT: We CANNOT take the absolute value here! Both sign AND magnitude matter.

Step-by-Step Example

Scenario: Soccer balls and basketballs. Demand: QD = 400 − 5P + 20PB, P = 20, PB = 15.

  • QD = 400 − 5(20) + 20(15) = 600
  • ∂Q/∂PB = 20
  • ε = (∂Q/∂PB) × (PB/Q) = 20 × (15/600) = 0.5
  • Positive sign → Substitutes

🔹 Income Elasticity of Demand

Definition: The income elasticity of demand measures the percentage change in quantity demanded in response to a 1% change in consumer income.

εr = %ΔQ / %ΔR
SignThe good is...Example
εr > 0Normal GoodYou buy more restaurant meals as income rises
εr < 0Inferior GoodYou buy less public transit as income rises (you buy a car instead)
0 < εr < 1NecessityBasic food, electricity
εr > 1LuxuryYachts, designer handbags
Example: Lilith's Income Cut in Half
  • Comics decreases: %ΔQ is negative, %ΔR is negative → εr is positive → Normal Good
  • Ramen increases: %ΔQ is positive, %ΔR is negative → εr is negative → Inferior Good

🔹 Price Elasticity of Supply

Definition: The price elasticity of supply measures the percentage change in quantity supplied in response to a 1% change in price.

εps = %ΔQs / %ΔP
⚠️ Sign: ALWAYS POSITIVE! The Law of Supply says: when price goes up, firms want to produce more.

🔹 The Critical Distinction: Elasticity vs. Slope

FeatureSlopeElasticity
DefinitionAbsolute change: ΔQ/ΔPPercentage change: %ΔQ/%ΔP
UnitsHas units (e.g., "apples per dollar")No units! It's a pure number
Linear DemandConstant across the entire curveVaries at every point
AdvantageSimple to calculateAllows comparison across any goods
⚠️ Key Exam Trick:
  • Slope = ΔQ/ΔP (constant for linear curves)
  • Elasticity = (ΔQ/Q)/(ΔP/P) = (ΔQ/ΔP) × (P/Q) (varies along the curve)

Key Insight: Even though a linear demand curve has a constant slope, elasticity varies at every point. At high prices, demand is elastic. At low prices, demand is inelastic. The midpoint is unit-elastic.

🔹 Total Revenue and Elasticity

Total Revenue (TR) = Price × Quantity = P × Q

If Demand Is...When Price IncreasesWhen Price Decreases
Elastic (|ε| > 1)Revenue DecreasesRevenue Increases
Unit Elastic (|ε| = 1)Revenue UnchangedRevenue Unchanged
Inelastic (|ε| < 1)Revenue IncreasesRevenue Decreases
⚠️ Memory Trick: "If you're elastic, raising prices is a disaster!" (Revenue falls). "If you're inelastic, raising prices is fantastic!" (Revenue rises).

🔹 Consumer Surplus & Producer Surplus

Consumer Surplus (CS): The difference between what consumers are willing to pay and what they actually pay.

Producer Surplus (PS): The difference between what producers receive and their minimum acceptable price.

CS = ½ × (Pmax − P*) × Q*
PS = ½ × (P* − Pmin) × Q*
Example: Pencil Market
  • Demand: QD = 120 − 2P (Pmax = 60)
  • Supply: QS = 4P − 48 (Pmin = 12)
  • Equilibrium: P* = 28, Q* = 64
  • CS = ½ × (60 − 28) × 64 = 1024
  • PS = ½ × (28 − 12) × 64 = 512
  • Total Surplus = 1024 + 512 = 1536

🧮 Key Formulas

Own-Price Elasticity: εd = %ΔQd / %ΔP
Cross-Price Elasticity: εpyx = %ΔQx / %ΔPy
Income Elasticity: εr = %ΔQ / %ΔR
Price Elasticity of Supply: εps = %ΔQs / %ΔP
Consumer Surplus: CS = ½ × (Pmax − P*) × Q*
Producer Surplus: PS = ½ × (P* − Pmin) × Q*
Total Surplus: TS = CS + PS
Total Revenue: TR = P × Q

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Elasticity with Slope: "A steep demand curve means demand is inelastic" — FALSE. Elasticity depends on both slope AND P/Q ratio.
  • Confusing Movement vs. Shift: "If income increases, we move along the demand curve" — FALSE. Income changes SHIFT the demand curve.
  • Incorrect Absolute Value for Cross-Price: "Cross-price elasticity is -0.5, so they are substitutes" — FALSE. Negative means COMPLEMENTS.
  • Mixing Up Classifications: "Normal goods have negative income elasticity" — FALSE. Normal goods have POSITIVE income elasticity.
  • Misapplying Revenue Rule: "If demand is elastic, increasing price increases revenue" — FALSE. Elastic demand → price ↑ → revenue ↓.
  • Forgetting the Law of Demand Sign: "Price elasticity of demand is positive" — FALSE. It's negative (we use absolute value for interpretation).
  • Confusing Luxury vs. Necessity: "Luxury goods have income elasticity between 0 and 1" — FALSE. Luxury goods have ε > 1.
  • Confusing Surplus: "Consumer surplus is the area above the demand curve" — FALSE. CS is BELOW demand and ABOVE market price.

The "If This, Then That" Logic

If...Then...
|ε| > 1Demand is ELASTIC → Price ↑ → Revenue ↓
|ε| < 1Demand is INELASTIC → Price ↑ → Revenue ↑
|ε| = 1Demand is UNIT-ELASTIC → Price ↑ → Revenue ↔
εr > 0Good is NORMAL → ε > 1 = Luxury, ε < 1 = Necessity
εr < 0Good is INFERIOR
εpyx > 0Goods are SUBSTITUTES
εpyx < 0Goods are COMPLEMENTS

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: The price of a good increases from $10 to $12, and quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80. What is the own-price elasticity of demand?
✅ Correct Answer: C
%ΔQ = (80-100)/100 = -20%
%ΔP = (12-10)/10 = 20%
ε = -20%/20% = -1.0
Q2: If demand is elastic (|ε| > 1), what happens to total revenue when price increases?
✅ Correct Answer: B
When demand is elastic, the quantity effect dominates. The percentage decrease in quantity is larger than the percentage increase in price, so total revenue decreases.
Q3: The price of Coke increases by 10%, and demand for Pepsi increases by 15%. What is the cross-price elasticity?
✅ Correct Answer: B
ε = 15%/10% = 1.5
Positive elasticity → Substitutes
Q4: Income increases by 5%, and demand for restaurant meals increases by 15%. What is the income elasticity?
✅ Correct Answer: B
εr = 15%/5% = 3.0
Since ε > 1, restaurant meals are a luxury normal good
Q5: Given QD = 100 − 2P and QS = 20 + 2P, find equilibrium and consumer surplus.
✅ Correct Answer: A
100 − 2P = 20 + 2P → 80 = 4P → P* = 20
Q* = 60
Demand intercept: Q=0 → P=50
CS = ½ × 60 × (50 − 20) = 400

📖 Glossary

Elasticity
Measure of responsiveness of one variable to changes in another.
Own-price elasticity
Percentage change in quantity demanded from a 1% increase in the good's own price.
Cross-price elasticity
Percentage change in quantity demanded of one good from a 1% increase in the price of another good.
Income elasticity
Percentage change in quantity demanded from a 1% increase in income.
Price elasticity of supply
Percentage change in quantity supplied from a 1% increase in price.
Consumer Surplus
Difference between willingness to pay and actual price paid.
Producer Surplus
Difference between price received and willingness to sell.
Total Surplus
Sum of consumer and producer surplus; CS + PS.
Total Revenue
Price × Quantity; total amount received from selling a good.
Substitutes
Goods where an increase in the price of one leads to an increase in demand for the other.
Complements
Goods where an increase in the price of one leads to a decrease in demand for the other.
Normal Good
Demand increases when income increases (positive income elasticity).
Inferior Good
Demand decreases when income increases (negative income elasticity).
Luxury Good
Normal good where demand increases more than proportionally to income (εr > 1).
Necessity Good
Normal good where demand increases less than proportionally to income (0 < εr < 1).
