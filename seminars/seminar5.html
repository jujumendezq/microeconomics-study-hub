🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to Seminar 5! This is one of the most exciting parts of microeconomics because we're moving from idealized markets (perfect competition) to real-world market structures where firms actually have market power.

Up until now, you've learned about perfectly competitive markets where firms are "price takers" – they have no control over the price and just accept whatever the market gives them. But in reality, most markets don't work that way! Think about your electric company, your local movie theater, or even universities competing for students. These firms have some control over their prices.

This seminar covers four big ideas:
  • Perfect Competition vs. Monopoly – Comparing the two extremes of market structure
  • Monopoly Pricing & Profit – How a single firm maximizes profit
  • Price Discrimination – Charging different prices to different customers
  • Game Theory & Oligopoly – How firms strategize when they're competing with just a few others

🔹 Perfect Competition in the Long Run

Definition: Perfect competition is a market structure where:

  • Many firms sell identical products
  • No single firm can influence the market price (they are "price takers")
  • Firms can freely enter or exit the market
  • In the long run, firms earn zero economic profit
Why Do Firms Earn Zero Profit in the Long Run?

If firms in a market are making profits, what happens? New firms enter because they see the profit opportunity. As more firms enter, the market supply increases, which drives the price down. This continues until profits are squeezed to zero.

Conversely, if firms are losing money, firms exit the market, supply decreases, prices rise, and losses disappear.

P = MC = AC (at minimum of AC)
Real-World Example: Think about farming wheat. If wheat farmers are making profits, more farmers will start growing wheat. This increases supply, lowers the price, and eventually profits disappear. In the long run, wheat farmers just break even.

🔹 Monopoly

Definition: A monopoly is a market structure where:

  • There is only one firm selling the product
  • There are barriers to entry that prevent other firms from entering
  • The monopolist is a price maker – they can choose the price
⚠️ Key Difference:
  • Competitive firm: Price = Marginal Revenue (P = MR)
  • Monopolist: Marginal Revenue < Price (MR < P)

Why? To sell an additional unit, the monopolist must lower the price on ALL units sold, not just the extra one.

Profit Maximization Rule: MR = MC
Example from Exercise 1:
  • TC = 16 + Q², Demand: Q = 24 − P
  • MR = 24 − 2Q, MC = 2Q
  • Set MR = MC: 24 − 2Q = 2Q → Q = 6
  • P = 24 − 6 = 18
  • π = TR − TC = (18 × 6) − (16 + 36) = 108 − 52 = 56
  • DWL = (18 − 12) × (8 − 6) / 2 = 6

🔹 Deadweight Loss

Definition: Deadweight loss is the loss of economic efficiency that occurs when the equilibrium for a good or service is not achieved. In monopoly, it's the value of trades that don't happen because the monopolist restricts output to raise the price.

Why Does Monopoly Create Deadweight Loss?
  • In perfect competition, we produce all units where consumers are willing to pay at least what it costs to produce them
  • In monopoly, the firm produces where MR = MC, which happens at a lower quantity and higher price
  • Some consumers who value the good more than its cost don't get it
  • Society loses the value of these "missing" trades
DWL = ½ × (PM − P*) × (QPC − QM)

🔹 The Lerner Index

Definition: The Lerner Index measures a firm's market power. It shows the percentage markup of price over marginal cost.

Lerner Index = (P − MC) / P = 1 / |ε|
If...Then...
Demand is very elastic (|ε| is large)The markup is small
Demand is inelastic (|ε| is small)The markup is large
Example from Exercise 8:
  • Market A: ε = −2 → Lerner Index = 1/2 → P = 2 × MC
  • Market B: ε = −4 → Lerner Index = 1/4 → P = 4/3 × MC
  • The firm charges a higher price in Market A because demand is less elastic

🔹 Price Discrimination

Definition: Price discrimination is when a firm charges different prices to different consumers for the same good or service, and the price differences are not based on cost differences.

DegreeDefinitionExample
First DegreeCharging each consumer exactly their maximum willingness to payPerfect negotiation, auctions
Second DegreeCharging different prices based on quantity purchased or versionBulk discounts, different quality versions
Third DegreeCharging different prices to different demographic groupsStudent discounts, senior discounts
Example from Exercise 6:
  • A movie theater has 900 students (WTP = 5 CHF) and 100 professors (WTP = 10 CHF)
  • MC = 3, no fixed costs
  • Price discrimination: Students pay 5 CHF, professors pay 10 CHF → Profit = 2,500
  • Uniform price of 10 CHF: Only professors buy → Profit = 700
  • Uniform price of 5 CHF: Everyone buys → Profit = 2,000

Result: Price discrimination maximizes profit!

⚠️ Conditions for Price Discrimination:
  • The firm must have market power
  • The firm must be able to identify different consumer groups
  • The firm must be able to prevent resale between groups (arbitrage)

🔹 Game Theory & Nash Equilibrium

Definition: Game theory is the study of strategic decision-making where the outcome depends on the choices of multiple players.

A Nash equilibrium is a set of strategies where no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing their strategy, given what the other players are doing.

TermDefinitionExample
Dominant StrategyA strategy that's best regardless of what others doWyoming always lowers tuition
Nash EquilibriumWhere no one wants to change their strategy given what others are doing(Montana raises, Wyoming lowers)
Prisoner's DilemmaWhere individual incentives lead to a worse outcome for bothCartel members cheating on agreements
Example from Exercise 11:

University of Montana and Wyoming choose tuition strategies:

Wyoming: RaiseWyoming: Lower
Montana: Raise(100, 40)(60, 80)
Montana: Lower(160, 80)(20, 120)
  • Wyoming's dominant strategy: Lower (80 > 40 and 120 > 80)
  • Montana's response: Given Wyoming lowers, Montana raises (60 > 20)
  • Nash Equilibrium: (Montana Raises, Wyoming Lowers)

🔹 Cartels

Definition: A cartel is a group of firms that agree to coordinate their production and pricing decisions to act as a monopoly and maximize joint profits.

⚠️ Why Are Cartels Unstable?
  • Each individual firm has an incentive to cheat
  • If one firm produces more than its agreed quantity, it can sell the extra output at the cartel price
  • This increases that firm's profit but decreases the cartel's total profit
  • If enough firms cheat, the cartel collapses
Key Insights from Exercises 9-10:
  • Cartel quantity = monopoly quantity < competitive quantity
  • Creating a cartel is optimal for firms (maximizes joint profits)
  • But cartels are unstable because each firm has an incentive to cheat
  • Consumer surplus is same as monopoly (higher than in perfect competition)

🧮 Key Formulas

Perfect Competition LR: P = MC = AC
Monopoly MR: MR = a − 2bQ (linear demand)
Profit: π = TR − TC = (P − AC) × Q
Lerner Index: (P − MC)/P = 1/|ε|
Deadweight Loss: DWL = ½ × (PM − PPC) × (QPC − QM)
Price Discrimination Profit: MR1 = MR2 = MC
Nash Equilibrium: No player can improve unilaterally

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Perfect Competition vs Monopoly

Scenario: TC = 16 + Q², market demand Q = 24 − P. Find both equilibria.

Solution:

  • Competition: P = MC = 2Q, AC = 16/Q + Q. MC = AC → Q = 4, P = 8, Qmarket = 8, n = 2 firms
  • Monopoly: MR = 24 − 2Q, MC = 2Q. MR = MC → Q = 6, P = 18, π = 56, DWL = 6

Scenario 2: Lerner Index Application

Scenario: A monopoly faces ε = −3, MC = 10. Find the optimal price.

Solution: Lerner Index = 1/|ε| = 1/3. (P − 10)/P = 1/3 → 3P − 30 = P → 2P = 30 → P = 15.

Scenario 3: Price Discrimination

Scenario: 900 students (WTP = 5 CHF), 100 professors (WTP = 10 CHF), MC = 3. Which strategy maximizes profit?

Solution: Price discrimination → Profit = 2,500. Uniform 10 CHF → Profit = 700. Uniform 5 CHF → Profit = 2,000. Price discrimination wins!

Scenario 4: Game Theory

Scenario: Two universities choose "Raise" or "Lower" tuition. Find the Nash equilibrium.

Solution: Wyoming has a dominant strategy to "Lower". Given Wyoming lowers, Montana raises (60 > 20). Nash Equilibrium: (Montana Raises, Wyoming Lowers).

Scenario 5: Cartels

Scenario: Which statement is correct about cartels?

Solution: Creating a cartel is optimal for firms (maximizes joint profits), but cartels are unstable because each firm has an incentive to cheat. Cartel quantity = monopoly quantity < competitive quantity.

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing q and Q: Use q for individual firm quantity, Q for total market quantity. Q = n × q.
  • Thinking P = MR in Monopoly: In monopoly, MR < P. Use MR = MC, then find P from demand.
  • Confusing Price Discrimination Degrees: Student discounts = Third-degree PD (observable group characteristics).
  • Assuming Cartels are Stable: Cartels are profitable but unstable because each firm has an incentive to cheat.
  • Misapplying the Lerner Index: Use absolute value of elasticity: |ε|. Lerner Index is always between 0 and 1.

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: A monopolist maximizes profit where:
✅ Correct Answer: B
All firms maximize profit where MR = MC. In monopoly, since MR < P, this means P > MC.
Q2: For a monopolist, MR is less than P because:
✅ Correct Answer: B
To sell more output, a monopolist must lower the price on ALL units sold, not just the additional unit. This is why MR < P.
Q3: The Lerner Index measures:
✅ Correct Answer: B
The Lerner Index = (P − MC)/P = 1/|ε|. It measures the markup over marginal cost as a percentage of price.
Q4: Student discounts are an example of:
✅ Correct Answer: C
Student discounts are third-degree price discrimination because they're based on observable group characteristics (student status).
Q5: Cartels are unstable because:
✅ Correct Answer: B
Cartels are unstable because each individual firm has an incentive to cheat by producing more than its agreed quantity, capturing a larger market share and higher profit.
