🧑‍🏫 Seminar 6: Externalities, Public Goods & Uncertainty

Market failures, public goods, and decision-making under risk

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Define externalities and distinguish between positive and negative externalities
  • Calculate the socially optimal quantity and optimal Pigouvian tax/subsidy
  • Identify the four types of goods based on excludability and rivalry
  • Distinguish between risk-averse, risk-neutral, and risk-seeking behavior
  • Calculate expected value and understand risk premiums
  • Apply these concepts to multiple-choice exam questions

🔹 Topic Introduction

This lesson covers three interconnected topics that explain why markets sometimes fail to produce the "right" amount of goods, and how individuals make decisions under uncertainty. Externalities occur when someone's actions affect others without being reflected in market prices—like pollution from factories or the benefits of getting an education. Public goods are things like clean air or national defense that everyone can use, but no one has an incentive to pay for. Uncertainty explores how people make choices when they don't know exactly what will happen—like whether to buy insurance or invest in Bitcoin.

Why should you care? These concepts explain why governments impose taxes on cigarettes, subsidize solar panels, and why you might buy insurance even though you hope you never need it. They're essential for understanding real-world policy debates and your own daily decisions.

🔹 Section A: Externalities

Concept 1: What is an Externality?

Definition: An externality is a cost or benefit from a transaction that affects a third party who didn't choose to be involved. The market price doesn't reflect this cost or benefit.

Real-World Examples:
  • Negative externality: A factory pollutes the air. The factory owner pays for materials and labor, but doesn't pay for the health problems caused to neighbors. The price of the factory's products doesn't include this "hidden" cost.
  • Positive externality: You get a flu vaccine. You benefit, but everyone around you also benefits because you're less likely to spread the flu. The price you pay doesn't reflect these extra benefits to others.
TypeEffectMarket OutcomeExample
Negative ExternalityExternal costOverproductionPollution, smoking
Positive ExternalityExternal benefitUnderproductionEducation, vaccines
⚠️ Key Insight: Externalities are a type of market failure—when the free market doesn't maximize social welfare. The government can "internalize" the externality with Pigouvian taxes (for negative externalities) or Pigouvian subsidies (for positive externalities).

🔹 Socially Optimal Quantity

Definition: The socially optimal quantity is the level of production that maximizes total social welfare—where marginal social benefit (MSB) equals marginal social cost (MSC).

MSC = MPC + External Cost
MSB = MPB + External Benefit
For Negative Externalities:
  • Private market: Q where Demand = Supply (too high!)
  • Social optimum: Q where MSB = MSC (lower than private equilibrium)
For Positive Externalities:
  • Private market: Q where Demand = Supply (too low!)
  • Social optimum: Q where MSB = MSC (higher than private equilibrium)
⚠️ Memory trick: "Socially Optimal = MSB = MSC" — Same letters as "SOM" (Socially Optimal is MSB = MSC).

🔹 Pigouvian Taxes and Subsidies

Pigouvian Tax: A tax on goods with negative externalities, designed to make the producer pay for the external cost.

Pigouvian Subsidy: A payment to encourage consumption/production of goods with positive externalities.

ScenarioTax/Subsidy Amount
Negative ExternalityTax = External Cost per unit (MSC - MPC)
Positive ExternalitySubsidy = External Benefit per unit (MSB - MPB)
Real-World Examples:
  • Pigouvian tax: A carbon tax on gasoline makes polluters pay for the environmental damage.
  • Pigouvian subsidy: Government rebates on electric vehicles encourage cleaner transportation.
⚠️ Memory trick: "Tax the Bad, Subsidize the Good" — Negative externalities get taxes; positive externalities get subsidies.

🔹 Section B: Public Goods

Concept 4: The Four Types of Goods

Goods can be classified based on two characteristics:

  • Excludability: Can people be prevented from using the good?
  • Rivalry: Does one person's use reduce availability for others?
TypeRival?Excludable?ExampleMarket Provides?
Private GoodYesYesPizza, clothing✓ Yes
Club GoodNoYesNetflix, cable TV✓ Yes
Common PoolYesNoFish in ocean, clean air✗ No (overused)
Public GoodNoNoNational defense, street lights✗ No (free-rider problem)
Key Insights:
  • Private goods (rival AND excludable): The market works!
  • Club goods (non-rival BUT excludable): The market can work, but these often have high fixed costs and low marginal costs.
  • Common pool goods (rival BUT non-excludable): The market fails because of the "tragedy of the commons."
  • Public goods (non-rival AND non-excludable): The market fails completely because of the "free-rider problem."
⚠️ Memory trick: "PRivate goods are Rival" — and both "Public" and "Pool" are Poorly provided by markets.

🔹 Section C: Uncertainty

Concept 5: Expected Value

Definition: Expected value (EV) is the average outcome of a risky situation, calculated by multiplying each possible outcome by its probability and summing the results.

EV = Σ(pi × Xi)

Where p₁ + p₂ + ... + pₙ = 1

Real-World Example: A lottery ticket has:
  • 90% chance of winning 0 CHF
  • 10% chance of winning 100 CHF
  • EV = (0.9 × 0) + (0.1 × 100) = 10 CHF

🔹 Risk Attitudes

People respond differently to risk based on their preferences:

TypeDefinitionFair Bet BehaviorUtility Function
Risk AversePrefers certainty; dislikes riskAlways refusesConcave
Risk NeutralOnly cares about expected valueIndifferentLinear
Risk SeekingEnjoys risk; likes gamblingAlways acceptsConvex

What is a "Fair Bet"?

A fair lottery has an expected value of zero. You pay a price equal to the expected payout.

Example: A coin toss where you pay 10 CHF to play:
  • Win 20 CHF if heads (gain +10)
  • Win 0 CHF if tails (gain -10)
  • EV = (0.5 × 10) + (0.5 × -10) = 0 → This is a fair bet!
  • Risk averse person: Refuses (prefers 10 CHF for sure)
  • Risk neutral person: Indifferent (EV = 0, so no advantage)
  • Risk seeking person: Accepts (enjoys the thrill)
⚠️ Risk Premium: The amount a risk-averse person would pay to avoid risk. For a risk-averse person, the risk premium is positive. For risk-seeking people, it's negative (they'd pay to gamble).

🔹 Prospect Theory

Definition: Prospect Theory (developed by Kahneman and Tversky) describes how people actually make decisions under risk, which differs from what economic models predict.

Key Findings

  • Overestimate low probabilities (e.g., buying lottery tickets)
  • Underestimate high probabilities (e.g., not wearing seatbelts)
  • Loss aversion: Feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains
⚠️ Exam connection: According to Prospect Theory, people tend to underestimate high probabilities. This is correct!

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Exercise 1 - Negative Externality

Scenario: Demand: QD = 7 − P, Supply: QS = P − 1, External cost = 5 CHF per unit.

Solution:

  • Private market: 7 − P = P − 1 → P = 4, Q = 3
  • MSC = (Q + 1) + 5 = Q + 6
  • Set MSB = MSC: 7 − Q = Q + 6 → QOpt = 0.5
  • Tax = 5 CHF per unit

Scenario 2: Exercise 2 - Positive Externality

Scenario: Demand: Qd = 48 − P, Supply: Qs = 16 + P, MSB = 56 − Q.

Solution:

  • Private market: 48 − P = 16 + P → P = 16, Q = 32
  • MSC = Q − 16 (from supply: Q = 16 + P → P = Q − 16)
  • Set MSB = MSC: 56 − Q = Q − 16 → QOpt = 36
  • Subsidy = MSB − MPB = 8 CHF per unit
  • Quantity increases from 32 to 36 → Increase of 4 units

Scenario 3: Exercise 3 - Alcohol Tax

Scenario: Demand: QD = 36 − P, Supply: QS = P, MSC = Q + 4.

Solution:

  • Private market: 36 − P = P → P = 18, Q = 18
  • MSC = Q + 4
  • Set MSB = MSC: 36 − Q = Q + 4 → QOpt = 16
  • Tax reduces quantity from 18 to 16 → Reduction of 2 units
  • Answer: (a) is correct

Scenario 4: Exercise 11 - Fertilizer

Scenario: Demand: PD = 84 − Q, Supply: PS = 0.2Q, Social supply: PSr = 0.2Q + 6.

Solution:

  • Private market: 84 − Q = 0.2Q → QMarket = 70
  • Social optimum: 84 − Q = 0.2Q + 6 → QOpt = 65
  • Difference = 5 tons
  • DWL = ½ × 5 × 6 = 15,000 francs
  • Answer: (d) is correct

Scenario 5: Exercise 10 - Bitcoin Decision

Scenario: Bitcoin currently 10,000 CHF. At 4 PM: 15,000 CHF (60%), 4,000 CHF (20%), 1,000 CHF (20%).

Solution:

  • EV = (0.6 × 15,000) + (0.2 × 4,000) + (0.2 × 1,000) = 10,000 CHF
  • Current price = 10,000 CHF = EV
  • Risk neutral person is indifferent
  • Answer: Only statement 3 is correct

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Private and Social Equilibrium: Negative externalities = market overproduces; positive externalities = market underproduces.
  • Tax vs. Subsidy Confusion: "Tax the Bad, Subsidize the Good."
  • Excludability vs. Rivalry: Draw the 2×2 matrix every time!
  • Risk Aversion and Fair Bets: Risk-averse individuals refuse FAIR bets (EV = 0). They might accept bets with positive expected value!
  • Probability Trees: Remember to multiply probabilities for sequential events.
