🔹 The Big Picture: Why This Chapter Matters

Welcome to the world of economics! Before we jump into supply, demand, and markets, we need to understand how economists think. This chapter is the foundation for everything else we'll learn this semester.

Think of it this way: If you're going to learn a new language, you first need to learn the grammar rules and vocabulary. In economics, this chapter provides exactly that—the "grammar" and "vocabulary" that economists use to understand the world.

Why should you care? Because economics isn't just about money or markets—it's a way of thinking that can be applied to almost any human problem. Want to understand why traffic jams happen? Why people smoke despite knowing it's bad for them? Why some countries are rich and others poor? Economics gives you the tools to figure out these puzzles.

By the end of this chapter, you'll understand:

  • What economics actually is (and isn't)
  • Why economists use models and what they're good for
  • The crucial difference between correlation and causality (this will save you from making logical errors!)
  • The fundamental principles that ALL economists agree on
  • How to think like an economist in everyday situations

🔹 Concept 1: What is Economics?

Economics is the study of how individuals, firms, and societies make choices when faced with scarcity.

Two Ways to Define Economics

Narrow Definition (Economics as a Discipline):

Economics focuses on decisions about money, markets, production, consumption, and resource allocation. It studies how people make choices about buying, selling, working, investing, and saving.

Broad Definition (Economics as a Method):

Economics is a way of thinking—a rigorous method of reasoning that can be applied to ANY human behavior or societal issue, not just "economic" ones. You can use economic thinking to study crime, education, health, marriage, religion, discrimination, sports, and even love!

Aspect Narrow View Broad View
Focus Markets, prices, production All human decision-making
Subjects Consumers, firms, governments Anyone who makes choices
Topics Inflation, trade, employment Crime, marriage, education, health
Method Formal economic models Economic reasoning applied anywhere
💡 Real-World Examples: Economists study not just stock markets and inflation, but also:
  • Crime: Why do people commit crimes? They weigh the costs (prison) against the benefits (money).
  • Education: Why do some students drop out of school? Opportunity cost—they could be earning money instead.
  • Marriage: People compare the benefits of being married (companionship, shared expenses) against the alternatives (being single).
  • Discrimination: Tastes and preferences influence hiring decisions.
⚠️ Exam tip: Understanding the broad view helps you see economics everywhere—it's not just about money! The narrow view gives us the specific tools we'll use in this course. Together, they show that economics is both a subject AND a way of thinking.

🔹 Concept 2: Key Economic Issues

Before diving into economic theory, it's important to understand what real-world problems economists are trying to solve.

1. Inflation

Definition: A general increase in the level of prices across the economy.

  • Impact: Reduces purchasing power—your money buys less
  • Recent Causes: Post-COVID supply shocks (shortages of raw materials) and geopolitical events (Ukraine war affecting energy prices)

2. Income Inequality

Definition: The unequal distribution of income across individuals or groups.

  • Between countries: The gap in average income between rich and poor nations
  • Within countries: The gap between rich and poor people in the same country
  • Measurement: The Gini coefficient is a statistical measure of income inequality (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality)

3. Climate Change

Definition: Long-term changes in global temperatures and weather patterns.

  • Economic Connection: Economic activity produces CO₂ emissions, which cause climate change; climate change then disrupts economic activity (a feedback loop)
  • Historical Context: CO₂ concentrations have increased exponentially since the Industrial Revolution

4. Protectionism

Definition: Government actions that restrict international trade (tariffs, quotas, etc.).

  • Historical Trend: Protectionism declined massively after WWII through GATT and WTO agreements
  • Recent Trend: A return to protectionism (e.g., Trump tariffs)

🔹 Concept 3: Microeconomics vs Macroeconomics

Microeconomics studies the behavior of individual economic agents—consumers, firms, workers, investors—and how they interact in specific markets.

Macroeconomics looks at the economy as a whole and studies aggregate variables like total production (GDP), inflation, unemployment, and economic growth.

Microeconomics Questions:

  • Why does the price of avocados go up in winter?
  • How does a firm decide how many workers to hire?
  • What happens to consumer demand when income increases?
  • How does a specific market reach equilibrium?

Macroeconomics Questions:

  • Why is inflation rising?
  • What causes unemployment?
  • How fast is the economy growing?
  • What is the total national debt?
Think of it like a forest:
  • Microeconomics looks at individual trees: How tall is this oak? How much sunlight does it need?
  • Macroeconomics looks at the entire forest: How many trees are there? Is the forest growing or shrinking? What's the overall health of the ecosystem?
⚠️ Exam tip: When asked whether something is micro or macro, ask yourself: "Is this about ONE person/firm/market or the WHOLE economy?"

🔹 Concept 4: Positive vs Normative Analysis

Positive Analysis is about "what IS." It describes how the world works, makes predictions, and generates testable claims. It is objective and based on facts.

Normative Analysis is about "what SHOULD BE." It makes value judgments and recommendations based on opinions, ethics, and beliefs. It is subjective.

Examples:
Positive: "A 10% increase in the minimum wage would reduce employment by 2% among low-skilled workers." (This can be tested with data)
Normative: "We should not raise the minimum wage because it hurts workers." (This is a value judgment)
Positive Analysis Normative Analysis
Describes cause and effect Expresses opinions and values
Testable with evidence Not testable—can't be proven right or wrong
"If X happens, then Y will happen" "We should/shouldn't..."
Example: "Higher taxes reduce consumer spending" Example: "We should raise taxes on the rich"
⚠️ Exam trick: Watch for words like "should," "ought to," "best," "worst"—these usually signal normative statements. Words like "will," "causes," "leads to" usually signal positive statements.

🔹 Concept 5: Economic Models

Economic models are simplified representations of reality that economists use to understand how the world works. They strip away unnecessary details to focus on the essential relationships between key variables.

Key Quote: "All models are wrong; some models are useful." (George Box)

What Models Do:

  • Simplify reality — focus on key elements, ignore noise
  • Isolate relationships — help identify cause and effect
  • Make predictions — tell us what will happen under certain conditions
  • Build counterfactuals — show what would have happened under different circumstances
💡 Think about a map:
  • A perfect 1:1 scale map would be the same size as the territory it's mapping—completely useless!
  • A good map simplifies: it shows roads but not every tree, or shows elevation but not every building.
  • Different maps serve different purposes (road map vs. topographic map vs. subway map).

Economic models work the same way. They highlight the MOST IMPORTANT factors and ignore the rest.

The Scientific Process in Economics:

  1. Observation: See something in the real world
  2. Generalization: Find regular patterns
  3. Model Building: Create a simplified representation
  4. Empirical Testing: Test model predictions against real data
⚠️ Exam trap: Don't reject a model because it's too simple! The point of models is SIMPLIFICATION, not perfect realism. Remember the map analogy!

🔹 Concept 6: Correlation vs Causality

Correlation is when two variables move together—when one changes, the other tends to change as well.

Causality is when one variable DIRECTLY CAUSES a change in another variable.

CRUCIAL POINT: Correlation DOES NOT imply causation! Just because two things happen together doesn't mean one caused the other.

This is probably the SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT concept in economics (and science in general). Mistaking correlation for causality leads to terrible policies, bad decisions, and logical errors.

Examples of Spurious Correlations:

Correlation Reality
Ice cream sales ↑, drownings ↑ Hot weather causes both
Number of pirates ↓, global temperature ↑ Just coincidence
Named cows ↑, milk production ↑ Farmer care (third variable)
Hospital stays ↑, deaths ↑ Sick people in hospitals
Police presence ↑, violent crime ↑ More crime → more police (reverse causality)

Why Variables Can Be Correlated Without Causation:

  • Reverse Causality: A causes B, not B causes A. Example: People in poverty have low education—but does low education cause poverty, or does poverty cause low education?
  • Third Variable (Confounding): Something else causes both. Example: Ice cream sales and drownings (hot weather causes both).
  • Coincidence: Sometimes things just happen together by chance. Example: The number of pirates and global temperature are correlated.
⚠️ Exam trap: When you see a statement like "X causes Y," ask yourself: Is this really causation, or just correlation? Could there be a third factor? Could the causation run the other way?

🔹 Concept 7: Counterfactuals and Establishing Causality

A counterfactual is the question: "What would have happened if the event being studied had NOT happened?"

Methods for Finding Counterfactuals:

1. Controlled Experiments

Create two groups that are identical in ALL characteristics:

  • Control Group: Not treated
  • Treatment Group: Receives the treatment
  • The ONLY difference is the treatment itself

Example: The Canadian "Return to Work" bonus. Random allocation of bonus to some workers. 15% positive effect on returning to work in the treatment group. Effect disappeared in the long term.

2. Natural Experiments

Use real-world events that happen "by chance" to create treatment and control groups.

Example: Minimum wage increase in New Jersey vs. Pennsylvania:

  • New Jersey increased minimum wage (treatment)
  • Pennsylvania kept it constant (control)
  • Finding: Employment increased or stayed constant (supporting non-competitive market theory)

3. Statistical/Econometric Techniques

  • Instrumental variables
  • Difference-in-differences analysis
  • Stratification
  • Monte Carlo experiments
Example: University Recordings Experiment. Students randomly given access to recordings or not. Identical groups except for recordings. Result: Recordings help strong students but hurt weak students.
⚠️ Exam trap: When you see an argument claiming that X causes Y, ask: "What is the counterfactual? What would have happened without X?"

🔹 Concept 8: Fundamental Principles — Optimization

Optimization (or rational choice) means that people make decisions by selecting the best available option given their circumstances.

At its core, economics assumes that people are rational—they make choices that they believe will make them as well off as possible, given their constraints. This doesn't mean people are perfect calculators or never make mistakes—it means they try to do what's best for themselves with the information they have.

Example: When you choose to study economics instead of going to a party, you're making a rational choice based on your preferences (you want good grades) and constraints (there's only so much time).

Two Key Concepts Related to Rationality:

  • Trade-offs: You can't have everything. Every choice means giving up something else.
  • Scarcity: Resources (time, money, etc.) are limited, forcing you to choose.
Real-World Application:
  • Why do university enrollments increase during economic crises? Opportunity cost of attending university is low (few jobs available).
  • Why do enrollments decrease during economic booms? Opportunity cost of attending university is high (good jobs available).

🔹 Concept 9: Opportunity Cost

Opportunity Cost is the value of the best alternative that you give up when you make a choice. It's the true cost of any decision—not just the money you spend, but everything you sacrifice.

Here's the problem with normal thinking about costs: we tend to only count the explicit costs (money). But economics teaches us that the TRUE cost of something is everything you could have done instead.
Economic Profit = Revenue − Economic Costs
Economic Costs = Accounting Costs + Opportunity Costs

Step-by-Step Example: Entrepreneurship Decision

An entrepreneur considers starting a business:

  • Revenue forecast: $240,000/year
  • Accounting costs (rent, materials, wages): $125,000/year
  • Accounting profit = $240,000 − $125,000 = $115,000
  • BUT the entrepreneur currently earns $160,000/year at their job (opportunity cost of their time)
  • Opportunity cost = $160,000
  • Economic costs = $125,000 + $160,000 = $285,000
  • Economic profit = $240,000 − $285,000 = −$45,000
Conclusion: The business LOOKS profitable ($115,000 accounting profit) but is actually a bad decision ($45,000 economic loss) because you'd be better off keeping your job!
⚠️ Exam trap: Watch for questions that ask about the "true cost" of something—they're almost certainly testing opportunity cost. Remember: the true cost includes BOTH explicit (money) AND implicit (opportunity) costs. There's no such thing as a "free lunch." Even if something is free in money terms, it still has an opportunity cost (your time, for example).

🔹 Concept 10: Thinking at the Margin

Thinking at the margin means making decisions by comparing the additional (marginal) benefits and additional (marginal) costs of a choice, rather than considering total benefits and total costs.

If Marginal Benefit > Marginal Cost → DO IT
If Marginal Benefit < Marginal Cost → DON'T DO IT
If Marginal Benefit = Marginal Cost → YOU'RE INDIFFERENT
Example: Should you drink another glass of water?
  • Total benefit from drinking ALL the water isn't what matters
  • What matters is: Is the benefit of THIS glass worth the cost?
  • Marginal Benefit: The additional benefit from consuming one more unit
  • Marginal Cost: The additional cost from consuming one more unit

Real-World Examples of Behavioral Responses to Incentives:

1. Seatbelts

When seatbelts became mandatory, deaths per accident fell, BUT people drove more recklessly because they felt safer.

Result: More accidents (but fewer deaths per accident). The marginal benefit of driving fast decreased (less dangerous).

2. Childcare Fines

A daycare center introduced a small fine for parents who picked up their children late.

Result: Instead of decreasing late pickups, the NUMBER OF LATE PICKUPS INCREASED! Parents now saw lateness as something they could "buy" (a service) rather than a moral obligation.

3. Congestion

Building more roads should reduce traffic, right? Actually, it often INCREASES traffic!

Result: People respond to lower congestion by driving more (and taking alternative routes less). This is called "induced demand."

4. COVID Vaccines

Upon introduction of vaccines, people became less careful—less mask-wearing, less social distancing.

Result: Increase in cases.

⚠️ Exam trick: When you see "marginal" in a question, think "additional" or "one more." Margins are about the NEXT unit, not the total.

🔹 Concept 11: Equilibrium

Equilibrium is a situation in which nobody would benefit by unilaterally changing their own behavior, given the behavior of others.

In markets, equilibrium occurs when:
  • Buyers are buying as much as they want at the current price
  • Sellers are selling as much as they want at the current price
  • There's no pressure for price to change

Think of it like a game of tug-of-war where both sides are equally matched—there's no movement.

  • Equilibrium occurs where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied
  • The equilibrium price is the price where this happens
  • Markets naturally tend toward equilibrium through price adjustments
  • Under the Right Conditions: Markets achieve an optimal outcome—you can't do better for aggregate welfare. This is known as "Pareto efficiency."

🔹 Concept 12: Market Failure

Market Failure occurs when markets fail to achieve an optimal outcome (maximize aggregate welfare) on their own. When markets fail, government intervention may improve outcomes.

Example: Pollution
  • A factory produces goods (beneficial) but also pollution (harmful)
  • The factory doesn't pay for the damage from pollution
  • Market price of the goods doesn't include the pollution cost
  • Result: Too much pollution and too much production

Sources of Market Failure:

  • Externalities: Costs or benefits that affect people not involved in the transaction
  • Market Power: When one firm dominates a market (monopoly)
  • Public Goods: Goods that are non-excludable and non-rivalrous (national defense, street lighting)
  • Imperfect/Asymmetric Information: When one party knows more than the other (used car markets, health insurance)
  • Incomplete Markets: Markets that simply don't exist for some things
⚠️ Key insight: Markets are powerful but not perfect. They fail in predictable ways, and governments can sometimes improve outcomes.

🧮 All Formulas (Explained Step-by-Step)

Formula 1: Economic Profit

Economic Profit = Total Revenue − Economic Costs
Economic Costs = Accounting Costs + Opportunity Costs
Variable Definition
Economic ProfitProfit after considering ALL costs (explicit and implicit)
Total RevenueMoney received from sales
Economic CostsAll costs including opportunity costs
Accounting CostsDirect, explicit costs (rent, materials, wages, etc.)
Opportunity CostsValue of the best forgone alternative

Step-by-Step Example: Should Sarah start a coffee shop?

  1. Accounting costs: Rent $2,000 + Materials $3,000 + Wages $4,000 = $9,000/month
  2. Estimated revenue: $15,000/month
  3. Accounting profit: $15,000 − $9,000 = $6,000/month
  4. Opportunity cost: Sarah currently earns $5,000/month working elsewhere
  5. Economic costs: $9,000 + $5,000 = $14,000/month
  6. Economic profit: $15,000 − $14,000 = $1,000/month
Result: Sarah should open the coffee shop! The business is economically profitable ($1,000 > $0).
⚠️ Exam trap: Exams might provide accounting profit and ask for economic profit. Remember to ADD the opportunity cost!

Formula 2: Marginal Decision Rule

If Marginal Benefit > Marginal Cost → DO IT
If Marginal Benefit < Marginal Cost → DON'T DO IT
If Marginal Benefit = Marginal Cost → YOU'RE INDIFFERENT

Step-by-Step Example: How many hours should you study?

  • After 2 hours: MB of 1 more hour = 5 points, MC = 1 hour of sleep → 5 > 1 → Study!
  • After 3 hours: MB of 1 more hour = 3 points, MC = 1 hour of sleep → 3 > 1 → Study!
  • After 4 hours: MB of 1 more hour = 1 point, MC = 1 hour of sleep → 1 = 1 → Indifferent (stop)
  • After 5 hours: MB of 1 more hour = 0 points, MC = 1 hour of sleep → 0 < 1 → STOP!
Result: The optimal study time is when marginal benefit = marginal cost (4 hours).
⚠️ Exam trap: Many students confuse "marginal" with "average" or "total." Remember: MARGINAL = ADDITIONAL or ONE MORE.

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Opportunity Cost

Scenario: Maria has a full-time job paying $50,000 per year. She's considering going back to university for a Master's degree. Tuition and fees will be $30,000 per year, and books and supplies will cost $5,000. The program takes 2 years.

Step-by-Step Analysis:

  • Direct Costs (Accounting): Tuition: $30,000 × 2 = $60,000; Books: $5,000 × 2 = $10,000 → Total explicit costs = $70,000
  • Opportunity Costs: Lost salary: $50,000 × 2 = $100,000
  • Total Economic Cost: $70,000 + $100,000 = $170,000
Answer: The true cost of Maria's degree is $170,000, not just the $70,000 she'll pay in tuition and fees.

Scenario 2: Positive vs Normative Analysis

Scenario: Consider these statements about the minimum wage:

  1. "Increasing the minimum wage to $15 will reduce employment among teenagers by 3%."
  2. "The minimum wage should be increased to $15 to help low-income workers."
  3. "States with higher minimum wages have higher unemployment rates."
  • Statement 1: POSITIVE (descriptive, testable, factual)
  • Statement 2: NORMATIVE (prescriptive, value judgment, uses "should")
  • Statement 3: POSITIVE (descriptive, testable)
Answer: Statements 1 and 3 are positive—they describe how the world IS. Statement 2 is normative—it prescribes how the world SHOULD BE.

Scenario 3: Correlation vs Causality

Scenario: A study finds that people who own pets live longer, on average, than people who don't own pets. A journalist concludes: "Owning a pet extends your lifespan. Everyone should get a pet!"

Possible Explanations:

  • Pets cause people to be healthier (less stress, more exercise) → Could be causation
  • Healthier people are more likely to get pets → Reverse causality
  • People who can afford pets also have better healthcare, better diets, etc. → Third variable (income/wealth)
Answer: The journalist is making a logical error by assuming correlation = causation. The correlation could be driven by reverse causality or a third variable.

Scenario 4: Reasoning at the Margin

Scenario: A theme park charges a flat fee for admission. For each ride, the park charges $5. You've already spent the admission fee and ridden 5 rides. The 6th ride requires another $5 and 10 minutes of waiting.

What MATTERS (Marginal Analysis):

  • Additional cost: $5 (ride fee) + 10 minutes of time
  • Additional benefit: The enjoyment of the 6th ride

What DOESN'T MATTER:

  • The admission fee (it's paid regardless—a "sunk cost")
  • The enjoyment from the first 5 rides (already obtained)
Answer: You should decide based on: "Is the enjoyment of ONE MORE ride worth the $5 and 10 minutes?"
⚠️ Exam trap: Watch for "sunk costs" (costs already incurred, can't be recovered). These should NOT influence decisions—only future marginal costs matter.

Scenario 5: Counterfactuals and Natural Experiments

Scenario: Two states in the US, New Jersey and Pennsylvania, are very similar in economic structure and population. In 1992, New Jersey increased its minimum wage in the fast-food sector from $4.25 to $5.05. Pennsylvania kept its minimum wage at $4.25. Economists Card and Krueger studied what happened to employment in the fast-food sector in both states.

  • New Jersey: Treatment group (increased minimum wage)
  • Pennsylvania: Control group (kept minimum wage constant)
  • The Counterfactual: What would have happened in New Jersey if the wage hadn't increased? Answer: The experience of Pennsylvania
  • The Findings: Employment in New Jersey increased or stayed constant
Answer: The natural experiment allowed economists to establish causality: the minimum wage increase DID NOT reduce employment in this case.

⚡ High-Yield Exam Focus (The "Night-Before" Summary)

Memorization Checklist

  • □ Economics: Study of choice under scarcity
  • □ Microeconomics: Individual agents and specific markets
  • □ Macroeconomics: Economy as a whole, aggregate variables
  • □ Positive Analysis: What IS (descriptive, testable)
  • □ Normative Analysis: What SHOULD BE (prescriptive, value judgments)
  • □ Model: Simplified representation of reality
  • □ Correlation: Two variables move together
  • □ Causality: One variable directly causes change in another
  • □ Opportunity Cost: Value of the best forgone alternative
  • □ Economic Profit: Revenue - (Accounting + Opportunity Costs)
  • □ Marginal: Additional/one more unit
  • □ Rationality: People choose the best available option given constraints
  • □ Equilibrium: No one can improve by changing behavior unilaterally
  • □ Market Failure: Markets fail to achieve optimal outcomes
  • □ Externalities: Costs/benefits affecting people not involved
  • □ Gini Coefficient: Measure of income inequality

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Correlation with Causation: "A and B happen together, so A causes B" — FALSE. They might be correlated, but causation requires more evidence.
  • Confusing Positive and Normative Analysis: Thinking value judgments are facts. "Should" = normative; "will" = positive.
  • Ignoring Opportunity Cost: Only counting explicit (money) costs. The TRUE cost includes implicit costs (time, forgone alternatives).
  • Confusing Micro and Macro: Micro = individual; Macro = aggregate/economy.
  • Forgetting Marginal Analysis: Making decisions based on totals or averages. Rational decisions compare ADDITIONAL (marginal) costs and benefits.
  • Thinking Models Must Be 100% Realistic: The point of models is SIMPLIFICATION, not perfect realism.
  • Counting Sunk Costs: Past expenses influence current decisions. Sunk costs can't be recovered; only future marginal costs matter.

Must-Know Calculations

  • Economic Profit: Revenue - (Accounting Costs + Opportunity Costs)
  • Marginal Decision Rule: MB > MC → DO IT; MB < MC → DON'T

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: Which of the following is a normative statement?
✅ Correct Answer: C
"Should" indicates a value judgment, making this normative. A, B, and D are positive statements that can be tested with data.
Q2: Maria gives up a $50,000/year job to start a business. Her accounting costs are $80,000/year and revenue is $120,000/year. What is her economic profit?
✅ Correct Answer: B
Accounting profit = $120,000 − $80,000 = $40,000
Opportunity cost = $50,000 (forgone salary)
Economic profit = $40,000 − $50,000 = −$10,000
Q3: A study finds that people who own pets live longer. A journalist concludes: "Owning a pet extends your lifespan." What is the error?
✅ Correct Answer: B
This is the classic correlation vs causation error. The correlation could be explained by:
• Healthier people being more able to care for pets (reverse causality)
• Wealthier people being able to afford pets AND healthcare (third variable)
Q4: Which of the following is a microeconomics question?
✅ Correct Answer: A
Microeconomics focuses on individual markets and agents. Avocado prices are a specific market question. B, C, and D are macroeconomics questions about the whole economy.
Q5: What is the opportunity cost of attending a concert if the ticket costs $50 and you could have worked 4 hours at $15/hour during that time?
✅ Correct Answer: C
Opportunity cost = Explicit cost ($50 ticket) + Implicit cost (4 hours × $15/hour = $60) = $110
