🌿 10. Externalities

Market failures, Coase theorem, Pigouvian taxes · spillover effects

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Define externalities and distinguish positive from negative
  • Explain why externalities represent a market failure
  • Understand the Coase theorem and its limitations
  • Analyze Pigouvian taxes and subsidies as policy solutions
  • Apply real-world examples: LONZA, glyphosate, traffic congestion, carbon taxes
  • Calculate the social optimum using marginal social cost and marginal social benefit

🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to the world of market failures! So far in this course, we've been studying perfectly competitive markets and assuming they work efficiently. But in the real world, markets often fail to achieve the optimal outcome. One of the most important reasons is externalities – the spillover effects of economic activity on third parties.

What is an externality?
An externality occurs when the production or consumption of a good affects someone who is not directly involved in the transaction, and this effect is not reflected in the market price.

Why should you care? Externalities explain some of the biggest problems facing society today:

  • Climate change – burning fossil fuels releases CO₂, which affects everyone
  • Traffic congestion – each driver ignores the delay they cause for others
  • Pollution – factories don't pay for the health costs of their emissions
  • Education – an educated population benefits everyone, not just the student

Understanding externalities helps us design better policies – like carbon taxes, congestion charges, and subsidies for education.

🔹 What is an Externality?

Definition: An externality is an uncompensated impact of one person's actions on another person's well-being. It occurs when the market price of a good does not reflect all the costs or benefits associated with its production or consumption.

TypeDefinitionExample
Negative Production ExternalityA firm's production imposes costs on others without payingFactory polluting a river, LONZA chemical plant
Negative Consumption ExternalityA person's consumption imposes costs on othersSmoking in public, driving during rush hour
Positive Production ExternalityA firm's production benefits others without rewardR&D that creates knowledge others can use
Positive Consumption ExternalityA person's consumption benefits others without rewardFlu vaccination, cycling to work
Real-World Example: LONZA Chemical Plant
LONZA produces chemicals in Switzerland. While the production creates jobs and valuable products, it also releases pollutants into the air and water. The local community bears the health and environmental costs, but LONZA doesn't pay for these damages. The market price of the chemicals doesn't reflect the pollution cost – this is a negative production externality.
⚠️ Key Insight: Externalities are a type of market failure because the market price doesn't reflect the full social cost or benefit of a good. This leads to overproduction (negative externalities) or underproduction (positive externalities) from society's perspective.

🔹 Negative Externalities (Cost Externalities)

Definition: A negative externality occurs when the production or consumption of a good imposes a cost on third parties. The social cost is greater than the private cost.

Marginal Social Cost (MSC) = Marginal Private Cost (MPC) + External Cost

The Problem: When there's a negative externality:

  • The firm only considers its private costs (MPC)
  • The firm ignores the external costs imposed on society
  • Result: The firm produces too much output at a price that's too low
  • The market equilibrium quantity (Qmarket) is greater than the socially optimal quantity (Qoptimal)
Real-World Example: Traffic Congestion
When you decide to drive during rush hour, you consider your private cost – the time and gas money it costs you. But you ignore the congestion cost you impose on other drivers (the time you add to their trips). This is a negative consumption externality. Everyone makes the same decision, leading to too many cars on the road during peak times.

Policy Solutions for Negative Externalities

  • Pigouvian Tax: A tax equal to the external cost. This internalizes the externality and shifts production to the socially optimal level.
  • Regulation: Government mandates limits on pollution, emissions, or production levels.
  • Property Rights: Clearly defining property rights can allow market-based solutions (Coase theorem).
  • Cap and Trade: Setting a cap on total pollution and allowing firms to trade pollution permits.
Real-World Example: Carbon Tax
Burning fossil fuels releases CO₂, which causes climate change – a classic negative externality. A carbon tax charges emitters for each ton of CO₂ released. This internalizes the climate cost, making fossil fuels more expensive and encouraging cleaner alternatives. Switzerland has implemented a CO₂ tax on heating fuels and has a separate levy on motor fuels.

🔹 Positive Externalities (Benefit Externalities)

Definition: A positive externality occurs when the production or consumption of a good provides a benefit to third parties. The social benefit is greater than the private benefit.

Marginal Social Benefit (MSB) = Marginal Private Benefit (MPB) + External Benefit

The Problem: When there's a positive externality:

  • The individual only considers their private benefit (MPB)
  • The individual ignores the external benefits to society
  • Result: The individual consumes too little of the good
  • The market equilibrium quantity (Qmarket) is less than the socially optimal quantity (Qoptimal)
Real-World Example: Education
When you get an education, you benefit from higher earnings and better job opportunities (private benefit). But society also benefits: a more educated workforce is more productive, pays more taxes, and contributes to innovation. The private benefit is less than the social benefit, leading to underinvestment in education.

Policy Solutions for Positive Externalities

  • Subsidies: Government payments to encourage consumption or production. This internalizes the positive externality and shifts consumption to the socially optimal level.
  • Public Provision: The government directly provides the good (e.g., public education, vaccines).
  • Vouchers: Government vouchers that individuals can use to purchase the good (e.g., education vouchers).
  • Information Campaigns: Encouraging consumption through public awareness (e.g., vaccination campaigns).
Real-World Example: Flu Vaccinations
When you get a flu shot, you protect yourself from the flu (private benefit). But you also protect others by reducing the spread of the virus (external benefit). The social benefit is higher than the private benefit, which justifies government subsidies for vaccinations.

🔹 The Coase Theorem

Definition: The Coase theorem states that if property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are low, private bargaining will lead to the efficient outcome regardless of who holds the property rights.

Key Quote: "The Coase theorem suggests that externalities can be solved through private negotiation without government intervention, as long as property rights are clear and bargaining is easy."

The Story: Fred and Anne

  • Fred grows apples and needs bees to pollinate his orchard.
  • Anne keeps bees that produce honey.
  • Anne's bees pollinate Fred's orchard, benefiting Fred.
  • This is a positive externality (Anne's bees help Fred).
  • Coase solution: Fred could pay Anne to keep more bees. Both benefit from the negotiation.
Another Example: Factory Pollution
  • Factory produces goods but emits pollution.
  • Neighbors suffer from the pollution (negative externality).
  • If property rights belong to neighbors: The factory must pay the neighbors to pollute. The factory will only pay if the value of production exceeds the cost of pollution.
  • If property rights belong to the factory: The neighbors must pay the factory to reduce pollution. The neighbors will only pay if the pollution cost exceeds the factory's cost of reducing pollution.
  • Result: The efficient outcome is reached either way!
⚠️ Limitations of the Coase Theorem:
  • High transaction costs: If many people are affected, bargaining is expensive and difficult.
  • Free-rider problems: People might try to benefit from the negotiation without contributing.
  • Information problems: Parties may not know the true costs and benefits.
  • Strategic behavior: Parties may hold out for a better deal.

🔹 Pigouvian Tax

Definition: A Pigouvian tax is a tax on a good or activity that generates negative externalities. The tax is set equal to the external cost per unit, which internalizes the externality and leads to the socially optimal outcome.

Pigouvian Tax = External Cost per Unit
Real-World Example: Carbon Tax
Burning fossil fuels releases CO₂, causing climate change. A carbon tax charges emitters for each ton of CO₂ released. This internalizes the climate cost, making fossil fuels more expensive and encouraging cleaner alternatives.

How a Pigouvian Tax Works

  • The tax adds to the firm's private cost, shifting the supply curve up (or left).
  • The new equilibrium quantity is the socially optimal quantity.
  • The tax revenue can be used to fund public goods or reduce other taxes.
Example: Garbage Tax (Pay-per-Bag)
Many cities charge households for each bag of garbage they throw away. This tax internalizes the cost of waste disposal, encouraging households to produce less waste and recycle more.
⚠️ Key Insight: A Pigouvian tax is different from other taxes because its purpose is to correct a market failure, not just to raise revenue. The optimal tax rate equals the external cost at the socially optimal quantity.

🔹 Real-World Examples

1. Traffic Congestion

  • Problem: Each driver ignores the delay they cause for other drivers during rush hour.
  • Negative externality: More cars on the road → longer travel times for everyone.
  • Solution: Congestion pricing (e.g., London's congestion charge, Singapore's ERP system). Drivers pay a fee to enter busy areas during peak hours.
  • Result: Fewer cars during peak times, faster travel, and revenue for public transportation.

2. Oklahoma Earthquakes

  • Problem: Wastewater injection from oil and gas drilling caused a dramatic increase in earthquakes in Oklahoma.
  • Negative externality: The drilling companies didn't pay for the damage caused by earthquakes.
  • Solution: Regulation limiting wastewater injection volumes.
  • Result: Earthquake frequency decreased after regulations were implemented.

3. Coffee Market Numerical Example

  • Demand: P = 10 − Q (represents willingness to pay)
  • Supply: P = Q (represents marginal private cost)
  • External cost: 2 per unit (e.g., pollution from coffee production)
  • Market equilibrium: Q = 5, P = 5
  • Social optimum: Q = 4, P = 6
  • Optimal Pigouvian tax: 2 per unit

Calculation: Set demand = supply + tax: 10 − Q = Q + 2 → 8 = 2Q → Q = 4. Price = 6. Tax revenue = 2 × 4 = 8.

4. Glyphosate and Health Concerns

  • Problem: Glyphosate (a herbicide) may have negative health effects on farmers and nearby communities.
  • Negative externality: The health costs are not reflected in the price of glyphosate.
  • Debate: Should glyphosate be banned, taxed, or regulated?
  • Policy options: Tax on glyphosate, restrictions on use, or subsidies for organic alternatives.

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Negative Externality Analysis

Scenario: A factory produces chemicals with MPC = 10 + Q, MSB = 60 − 0.5Q, and external cost = 2 per unit. Find the market equilibrium and social optimum.

Solution:

  • Market equilibrium: Set MSB = MPC → 60 − 0.5Q = 10 + Q → 50 = 1.5Q → QM = 33.33
  • Social optimum: Set MSB = MSC → MSC = MPC + 2 = 12 + Q → 60 − 0.5Q = 12 + Q → 48 = 1.5Q → QOpt = 32
  • DWL = ½ × (33.33 − 32) × 2 = 1.33

Scenario 2: Coase Theorem Application

Scenario: A farmer's bees pollinate a neighboring orchard. The orchard owner values pollination at $10,000/year. The bee-keeper can produce more honey with an additional hive costing $3,000/year. What happens?

Solution: The orchard owner should pay the bee-keeper $3,000−$10,000 to add the hive. Both benefit. If property rights are well-defined and transaction costs are low, this solution will emerge through private bargaining.

Scenario 3: Pigouvian Tax

Scenario: Demand: Q = 100 − 2P, Supply: Q = 20 + 3P, external cost = 5 per unit. Find the optimal tax and new equilibrium.

Solution:

  • Market equilibrium: 100 − 2P = 20 + 3P → 80 = 5P → PM = 16, QM = 68
  • Inverse demand: P = 50 − 0.5Q, Inverse supply: P = (Q − 20)/3
  • Social optimum: 50 − 0.5Q = (Q − 20)/3 + 5 → 50 − 0.5Q = (Q − 5)/3 → 150 − 1.5Q = Q − 5 → 155 = 2.5Q → QOpt = 62
  • Optimal tax = 5, PD = 50 − 0.5(62) = 19, PS = 19 − 5 = 14

Scenario 4: Positive Externality

Scenario: Flu vaccinations have MPB = 50 − Q, MC = 10 + 0.5Q, external benefit = 10 per vaccination. Find the market equilibrium and social optimum.

Solution:

  • Market equilibrium: 50 − Q = 10 + 0.5Q → 40 = 1.5Q → QM = 26.67
  • Social optimum: MSB = MPB + 10 = 60 − Q → 60 − Q = 10 + 0.5Q → 50 = 1.5Q → QOpt = 33.33
  • Optimal subsidy = 10

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Private vs. Social Costs: Private costs are borne by the producer; social costs include external costs. MSC = MPC + external cost.
  • Confusing Private vs. Social Benefits: Private benefits are enjoyed by the consumer; social benefits include external benefits. MSB = MPB + external benefit.
  • Coase Theorem Limitations: The Coase theorem only works when property rights are clear and transaction costs are low. When many people are affected, the theorem breaks down.
  • Pigouvian Tax vs. Regulation: A Pigouvian tax incentivizes firms to reduce pollution up to the point where the cost of reduction equals the tax. Regulation sets a fixed limit.
  • Overproduction vs. Underproduction: Negative externalities → overproduction. Positive externalities → underproduction.

📖 Glossary

Externality
An uncompensated impact of one person's actions on another's well-being.
Negative Externality
An externality that imposes a cost on others.
Positive Externality
An externality that provides a benefit to others.
Marginal Social Cost (MSC)
The total cost to society of producing one more unit = MPC + external cost.
Marginal Private Cost (MPC)
The cost to the producer of producing one more unit.
Marginal Social Benefit (MSB)
The total benefit to society of consuming one more unit = MPB + external benefit.
Marginal Private Benefit (MPB)
The benefit to the consumer of consuming one more unit.
Coase Theorem
Private bargaining leads to efficiency if property rights are clear and transaction costs are low.
Pigouvian Tax
A tax equal to the external cost of a negative externality.
Pigouvian Subsidy
A subsidy equal to the external benefit of a positive externality.
Market Failure
When markets fail to allocate resources efficiently.
Transaction Costs
The costs of making an economic exchange.
Social Optimum
The quantity where MSB = MSC.
Deadweight Loss
The loss in total surplus from market inefficiency.
Overproduction
Producing more than the socially optimal quantity.
Underproduction
Producing less than the socially optimal quantity.
