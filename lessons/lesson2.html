📊 2. The Market: When Supply Meets Demand

Demand, supply, equilibrium · the core of microeconomics

🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to one of the most important chapters in all of economics! This chapter introduces you to the market—the central organizing mechanism of our economy. Think of a market as a meeting place (not necessarily physical) where buyers and sellers come together to exchange goods and services.

Why should you care? Because markets are everywhere! Every time you buy a coffee, sell your old textbooks, or even bid on a candy bag in class, you're participating in a market. Understanding how markets work helps you understand why prices rise and fall, why some products are abundant while others are scarce, and how governments can influence the economy through policies like taxes or price controls.

By the end of this chapter, you'll have the fundamental tools to analyze any market—from the market for smartphones to the market for oil to the market for labor. These tools will serve as the foundation for almost everything else you'll learn in microeconomics.

🔹 What is a Market?

A market is a group of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service. Markets can be:

  • Physical: like a farmer's market or a car dealership
  • Virtual: like Amazon, eBay, or a stock exchange
  • Abstract: like the market for oil or foreign currency

Key point: Markets don't require a physical location. What matters is that buyers and sellers can interact to exchange goods or services.

Side Who What They Do
Demand Side Buyers (consumers) Determine how much they want to purchase at different prices
Supply Side Sellers (producers) Determine how much they want to sell at different prices
The "Invisible Hand"
When buyers and sellers interact, they collectively determine the price of the good. This happens through a process of negotiation, bargaining, and competition—even if we can't "see" it happening. Adam Smith called this the invisible hand of the market.

🔹 The Demand Curve

Demand refers to the quantity of a good or service that buyers are willing and able to purchase at various prices.

The Law of Demand states: There is an inverse (negative) relationship between price and quantity demanded—as the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded decreases, ceteris paribus (all else equal).

The "Why" Behind the Law of Demand

  • The Substitution Effect: When the price of a good rises, it becomes relatively more expensive compared to other goods. Consumers "substitute" away from the more expensive good toward cheaper alternatives.
    Example: If the price of coffee increases, you might switch to drinking tea instead.
  • The Income Effect: When the price of a good rises, your purchasing power decreases—your money doesn't go as far. With effectively less income, you'll buy less of the good.
    Example: If the price of gasoline doubles, you might drive less and take the bus more often.

🔹 Individual vs. Market Demand

Individual Demand Curve: Each person has their own demand curve based on their preferences, income, and needs. These curves are heterogeneous—they differ from person to person.

Example: At $6, Person A wants 10 units, Person B wants 0 units.

Market Demand Curve: The market demand curve is the horizontal sum of all individual demand curves. For each price, we add up how many units all consumers want.

Important observation: The market demand curve is flatter (more elastic) than individual curves because we're adding up quantities at each price.

🔹 Factors That Shift Demand

⚠️ Movement Along vs. Shift: The Crucial Difference
Movement Along the Curve Shift of the Curve
What causes it? Change in the good's OWN price Change in other factors (income, tastes, prices of related goods)
What it's called Change in quantity demanded Change in demand
Visual Moving from one point to another on the SAME curve The ENTIRE curve moves left or right

Factors That Shift Demand (The Determinants of Demand)

  • Tastes and Preferences (T): Change in consumer preferences → shift in demand
    Example: A medical lecture on sugar's health risks shifts candy demand LEFT
  • Income and Wealth (R):
    • Normal goods: As income increases, demand increases (shift RIGHT)
    • Inferior goods: As income increases, demand decreases (shift LEFT)
  • Prices of Related Goods:
    • Substitutes: Goods used in place of each other. If the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original good increases (shift RIGHT)
    • Complements: Goods used together. If the price of a complement rises, demand for the original good decreases (shift LEFT)
  • Number of Buyers: More buyers → demand shifts RIGHT; Fewer buyers → demand shifts LEFT
  • Expectations About the Future: Expecting higher future prices → buy more NOW (shift RIGHT); Expecting lower future prices → buy less NOW (shift LEFT)

Substitutes vs. Complements: Detailed Examples

Substitutes (replace each other)

  • Coca-Cola and Pepsi
  • Tea and Coffee
  • Cars and Bikes

Example: Government introduces a CO₂ tax on gasoline → price of driving cars increases → demand for (electric) bikes shifts RIGHT.

Complements (used together)

  • Cars and Gasoline
  • Smartphones and Phone Cases
  • Coffee and Sugar
  • Flights and Holidays

Example: CO₂ tax increases flight prices → demand for holidays in faraway islands shifts LEFT.

🔹 The Supply Curve

Supply refers to the quantity of a good or service that sellers are willing and able to sell at various prices.

The Law of Supply states: There is a positive (direct) relationship between price and quantity supplied—as the price of a good increases, the quantity supplied increases, ceteris paribus.

The "Why" Behind the Law of Supply

  • Higher prices cover higher costs: As firms produce more, they face increasing marginal costs. They need higher prices to justify producing additional units.
  • Profit motive: Higher prices mean higher potential profits, which incentivizes firms to produce and sell more.
  • Opportunity cost: Sellers require compensation for giving up their goods. The higher the price, the more sellers are willing to part with their goods.

🔹 Factors That Shift Supply

Movement Along the Curve Shift of the Curve
What causes it? Change in the good's OWN price Change in other factors (production costs, technology, etc.)
What it's called Change in quantity supplied Change in supply
Visual Moving along the SAME curve The ENTIRE curve moves left or right

Factors That Shift Supply

  • Input Prices / Production Costs: Costs go UP → supply shifts LEFT (less profitable to produce); Costs go DOWN → supply shifts RIGHT (more profitable to produce)
    Example: Price of lithium increases → smartphone supply shifts LEFT
  • Technology: Better technology → lower costs → supply shifts RIGHT
    Example: Fracking technology → oil supply shifts RIGHT
  • Number of Sellers: More sellers → supply shifts RIGHT; Fewer sellers → supply shifts LEFT
  • Sellers' Expectations: Expect higher future prices → supply shifts LEFT (hold back inventory); Expect lower future prices → supply shifts RIGHT (sell more now)
  • Prices of Other Goods: If the price of an alternative good rises, producers shift production to that good

🔹 Market Equilibrium

Market equilibrium occurs when the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied at a particular price. This price is called the equilibrium price (or market-clearing price), and the corresponding quantity is the equilibrium quantity.

At equilibrium: QD = QS

At equilibrium:

  • Every buyer willing to pay the equilibrium price can find a seller
  • Every seller willing to accept the equilibrium price can find a buyer
  • There is no pressure for the price to change

The Adjustment Mechanism

Case 1: Excess Supply (Surplus)

  • When price is ABOVE equilibrium:
  • Quantity supplied > Quantity demanded
  • Sellers have unsold goods (inventories build up)
  • Sellers compete by lowering prices
  • Price falls until equilibrium is reached

Case 2: Excess Demand (Shortage)

  • When price is BELOW equilibrium:
  • Quantity demanded > Quantity supplied
  • Consumers can't find enough goods
  • Consumers compete by bidding prices up
  • Price rises until equilibrium is reached
⚠️ The Short Side of the Market: In a disequilibrium situation, the short side of the market prevails—the actual quantity exchanged is the smaller of quantity demanded and quantity supplied.

🧮 Key Formulas

QD = a − bP (Linear Demand)
QS = c + mP (Linear Supply)
At equilibrium: a − bP = c + mP → solve for P*, then Q*

Step-by-Step Example

Given: QD = 200 − 10P, QS = −40 + 50P

  1. Set equal: 200 − 10P = −40 + 50P
  2. Add 40: 240 − 10P = 50P
  3. Add 10P: 240 = 60P
  4. Divide: P* = 4
  5. Substitute: Q* = 200 − 10(4) = 160
Answer: Equilibrium price is $4, equilibrium quantity is 160 units.
Complete demand: QD = a − b₁P + b₂R + b₃PC + b₄PS
Complete supply: QS = c + m₁P − m₂W + m₃τ
Indirect Demand Function: P = a/b − (1/b)QD

Example: QD = 200 − 10P → P = 20 − 0.1QD

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Demand Shift (Candy Demand)

Scenario: A class runs an auction for a candy bag. The highest bid is 500 million CHF. If students attend a medical lecture about the dangers of sugar before the auction, what happens to the demand curve?

Solution: The demand curve shifts LEFT. Students are now willing to pay less for the candy bag at every price. Result: P ↓, Q ↓.

Scenario 2: Supply Shift (Smartphones)

Scenario: You're selling your smartphone in class. Lithium (a key component in smartphone batteries) just became 50% more expensive. What happens to the supply curve?

Solution: The supply curve shifts LEFT. At any price, sellers are willing to supply fewer smartphones because production costs are higher. Result: P ↑, Q ↓.

Scenario 3: Substitutes vs Complements

Scenario: The government introduces a CO₂ tax that makes driving cars more expensive. How does this affect the demand for (electric) bikes and the demand for holidays in faraway islands?

Solution:

  • Bikes (substitutes): Demand shifts RIGHT → P ↑, Q ↑
  • Holidays (complements): Demand shifts LEFT → P ↓, Q ↓

Scenario 4: Equilibrium Analysis with Income Change

Scenario: Coffee market at P=$4, Q=160. Income increases from $5,000 to $7,000. Given: QD = 200 − 10P + 0.03R, QS = −40 + 50P

Solution:

  • Initial: QD = 200 − 10P + 0.03(5000) = 350 − 10P
  • New: QD = 200 − 10P + 0.03(7000) = 410 − 10P
  • Set equal: 410 − 10P = −40 + 50P → 450 = 60P → P* = 7.5
  • Q* = 410 − 10(7.5) = 335
Result: P ↑ from $4 to $7.50, Q ↑ from 160 to 335.

Scenario 5: Simultaneous Shifts

Scenario: Smartphone market experiences TWO changes: income increases (normal good) and production technology improves. What happens to equilibrium?

Solution:

  • Income ↑ → Demand shifts RIGHT (P ↑, Q ↑)
  • Technology ↑ → Supply shifts RIGHT (P ↓, Q ↑)
  • Net effect: Q ↑ definitely; P is AMBIGUOUS
Answer: Quantity increases for sure; price depends on which shift is larger.

Common Exam Traps

  • Movement vs. Shift: Price change = movement along the curve. Anything else (income, tastes, etc.) = shift of the curve.
  • Normal vs. Inferior: Income ↑ → Normal good demand ↑ (RIGHT); Inferior good demand ↓ (LEFT).
  • Substitutes vs. Complements: Substitute price ↑ → demand for other ↑; Complement price ↑ → demand for other ↓.
  • Supply Shift Direction: Input costs ↑ → supply LEFT; Technology ↑ → supply RIGHT.
  • Simultaneous Shifts: If both D and S shift, one effect is ambiguous (price or quantity).

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: If the price of a good increases, what happens to quantity demanded?
✅ Correct Answer: B
According to the Law of Demand, there is an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded. When price increases, quantity demanded decreases.
Q2: Coffee and tea are substitutes. If the price of coffee increases, what happens to the demand for tea?
✅ Correct Answer: B
When the price of a substitute increases, consumers switch to the alternative, causing demand for the other good to increase (shift RIGHT).
Q3: A technological breakthrough reduces production costs for smartphones. What happens to equilibrium?
✅ Correct Answer: C
Lower production costs shift supply to the RIGHT. At any given price, more is supplied, leading to a lower equilibrium price and higher equilibrium quantity.
Q4: Given QD = 100 − 5P and QS = 20 + 3P, what is the equilibrium price and quantity?
✅ Correct Answer: B
100 − 5P = 20 + 3P → 80 = 8P → P* = 10 → Q* = 100 − 5(10) = 50
Q5: If both demand and supply increase simultaneously, what happens to equilibrium?
✅ Correct Answer: C
When both demand and supply increase, quantity definitely increases. Price is ambiguous—it depends on which shift is larger.

📖 Glossary

Market
A group of buyers and sellers of a particular good or service.
Demand
The quantity of a good consumers are willing and able to buy at various prices.
Supply
The quantity of a good producers are willing and able to sell at various prices.
Quantity Demanded
The amount bought at a SPECIFIC price.
Quantity Supplied
The amount sold at a SPECIFIC price.
Law of Demand
Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions (inverse relationship).
Law of Supply
Price and quantity supplied move in the same direction (direct relationship).
Equilibrium
Price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied.
Excess Demand
Demand > Supply (shortage).
Excess Supply
Supply > Demand (surplus).
Substitutes
Goods used in place of each other (Coffee and Tea).
Complements
Goods used together (Coffee and Sugar).
Normal Good
Demand increases when income increases.
Inferior Good
Demand decreases when income increases.
Movement Along the Curve
Change in quantity due to price change.
Shift of the Curve
Change in demand/supply due to other factors.
Horizontal Summation
Adding quantities at each price to get market curve.
Short Side of the Market
In disequilibrium, the smaller of QD and QS determines trade.
Ceteris Paribus
"All else equal" — assumption that other variables are held constant.
