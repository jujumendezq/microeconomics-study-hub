⚖️ 5. Government Interventions in Perfectly Competitive Markets

Price ceilings, price floors, and the efficiency-equity trade-off

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why governments might adopt policies that move markets away from the optimum
  • Identify the losers and winners from policy interventions
  • Evaluate the welfare effects of price controls (ceilings and floors)
  • Understand the concept of tax incidence
  • Figure out the link between tax incidence and the elasticities of demand and supply
  • Compute the deadweight loss of a tax
  • Compare price controls versus subsidies as policy alternatives
  • Provide real-life examples of price ceilings and price floors

🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to one of the most practically relevant chapters in microeconomics! In the previous chapter, you learned that competitive markets naturally lead to a Pareto optimal outcome—meaning no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off. So why on earth would governments mess with this beautiful efficiency?

This chapter reveals the "why" behind government interventions. Governments intervene in markets for reasons of fairness, equity, and political pressure—even when it means sacrificing some economic efficiency. You'll learn to analyze the winners and losers of policies like rent control, minimum wage, taxes, and subsidies. By the end, you'll be able to evaluate the welfare effects of these interventions and calculate deadweight loss—a concept that will appear repeatedly throughout your economics studies.

Why should you care? Because you've likely encountered these policies in your daily life: soaring rents in Geneva, debates about raising the minimum wage, the VAT you pay on every purchase, and price spikes during Uber surges. Understanding these tools will help you evaluate political debates and real-world policy decisions with a critical, economist's eye.

🔹 Why Governments Intervene in Markets

Definition: Government intervention occurs when the state uses laws, regulations, or fiscal tools to influence market outcomes—typically when it deems the equilibrium price "unfair" or "unsuitable."

The Rationale

Even though competitive markets deliver Pareto optimal outcomes, governments intervene for several reasons:

  • Fairness/Equity Concerns: The market may deliver efficient outcomes but with unacceptable distributional consequences. For example: rent that's too high for low-income families, wages too low for workers to live on, interest rates that are exploitative.
  • Market Power: When one side of the market has too much power (monopoly or monopsony), the market is no longer competitive, and intervention can actually improve efficiency.
  • Lobbying and Rent-Seeking: Powerful interest groups pressure politicians to create policies that benefit them, often at the expense of others.
  • Perceived Cost-Effectiveness: Sometimes governments believe price controls are less costly than subsidies (even though subsidies may be more efficient overall).
⚠️ Key point: The government is NOT intervening to solve a "market failure" in this chapter—we're assuming the starting point IS a competitive market. (Market failures like externalities come later in the course!)
Real-World Examples:
  • Rent Control: Cities like Geneva, New York, and San Francisco cap how much landlords can charge
  • Minimum Wage: Many countries set a legal floor on hourly wages
  • Usury Laws: Limits on interest rates that lenders can charge
  • Agricultural Price Supports: Governments guarantee minimum prices for crops

🔹 Price Ceilings

Definition: A price ceiling is a legal maximum price at which a good or service can be sold. It makes it illegal to charge a price higher than the ceiling.

The "Why": Governments impose price ceilings to protect consumers from prices they consider unfairly high (e.g., housing rents, essential goods during emergencies).

The Catch: For a price ceiling to have any effect, it must be binding—meaning it's set below the equilibrium price. If it's above equilibrium, it's non-binding and has no effect.

TypeConditionEffect
Binding Price CeilingSet below equilibriumCreates shortage (excess demand)
Non-Binding Price CeilingSet above equilibriumNo effect; market clears at equilibrium

The Mechanism

Imagine the equilibrium rent for an apartment is CHF 2,000/month. The government sets a price ceiling (rent control) at CHF 1,500/month.

  • Demand: At CHF 1,500, MORE people want apartments (quantity demanded increases)
  • Supply: At CHF 1,500, FEWER landlords want to rent out apartments (quantity supplied decreases)
  • Result: Excess demand (shortage) = QD − QS > 0
⚠️ How Quantity Is Determined: When there's a shortage, the short side of the market prevails—the quantity traded is the quantity supplied because you can't force sellers to provide more at the lower price. So Q = QS.
Real-World Example: Rent Control in Geneva
  • Landlords cannot charge rent higher than the average of equivalent apartments in the neighborhood
  • Result: Geneva has one of the lowest vacancy rates in Switzerland (below 2%, which signals a severe shortage)
  • Consequence: People stay in apartments they no longer need (e.g., older couples in large apartments after children move out) because they can't find smaller, affordable alternatives

Short-Run vs. Long-Run Effects (CRUCIAL!)

FactorShort RunLong Run
Supply ElasticityInelastic (few new buildings, limited changes)Elastic (developers can build less, landlords can convert to other uses)
Demand ElasticityInelastic (housing is a necessity, limited mobility)Elastic (people can move to other cities, search more)
ShortageSmallerLARGER (because both curves are more elastic)

Why it matters: The shortage created by rent control worsens over time as landlords reduce maintenance (quality deteriorates), fewer new housing units are built (lower profitability), and low rents attract more people to the city (increased demand).

🔹 Price Floors

Definition: A price floor is a legal minimum price at which a good or service can be sold. It makes it illegal to charge a price lower than the floor.

The "Why": Governments impose price floors to protect producers/sellers from prices they consider unfairly low (e.g., agricultural products, labor wages).

The Catch: For a price floor to have any effect, it must be binding—meaning it's set above the equilibrium price. If it's below equilibrium, it's non-binding and has no effect.

TypeConditionEffect
Binding Price FloorSet above equilibriumCreates surplus (excess supply)
Non-Binding Price FloorSet below equilibriumNo effect; market clears at equilibrium

The Mechanism

Imagine the equilibrium wage for fast-food workers is CHF 18/hour. The government sets a minimum wage (price floor) at CHF 20/hour.

  • Supply (Workers): At CHF 20, MORE people want to work (quantity supplied increases)
  • Demand (Firms): At CHF 20, FEWER workers are hired (quantity demanded decreases)
  • Result: Excess supply (unemployment) = QS − QD > 0
⚠️ How Quantity Is Determined: When there's a surplus, the short side of the market prevails—the quantity traded is the quantity demanded because you can't force firms to hire more workers at the higher wage. So Q = QD.
Real-World Example: Minimum Wage
  • History: As early as 1524 in UK towns; national laws in New Zealand (1894), Australia (1896), UK (1909)
  • Switzerland: No federal minimum wage—negotiations at canton/sector level
  • Concern: Minimum wage laws are often proposed for low-skilled workers (high-skilled workers typically earn above minimum wage anyway)

The Monopsony Counter-Argument (IMPORTANT!)

  • In perfectly competitive labor markets, minimum wages create unemployment
  • But in monopsony markets (where employers have market power as buyers of labor), the introduction or increase of a minimum wage can actually increase employment!
Famous Evidence:
  • Card & Krueger (1994): Studied New Jersey's minimum wage increase (from $4.25 to $5.05) compared to Pennsylvania (no increase). Found no negative effect on employment—actually, employment increased!
  • Card won the 2021 Nobel Prize for this and other empirical contributions to labor economics
  • Germany (2015): Introduction of a €8.50 minimum wage → no evidence of job losses; instead, it boosted pay for low-wage workers without harming employment

🔹 Taxes

Definition: A tax is a compulsory financial charge imposed by the government on individuals or transactions, without any direct counterpart benefit from the state.

Two Types of Taxation

TypeDefinitionExample
Direct TaxationPersonal tax depending on individual/entity financial situationIncome tax, wealth tax (paid in June in Switzerland)
Indirect TaxationTax on market transactions, independent of incomeVAT (Value Added Tax), sales tax, excise taxes (cigarettes, alcohol)

Swiss VAT Rates (as of 1.1.2018)

  • General: 7.7% (lower than neighboring countries)
  • Essential goods (food, books, water): 2.5%
  • Hotel accommodation: 3.7%

Key Concepts for Indirect Taxation

  • Tax Burden (Deadweight Loss): The reduction in total surplus caused by the tax. When a tax is imposed, it creates a distortion—quantity traded decreases, and society loses the surplus from the units that are no longer traded.
  • Tax Incidence: How the burden of the tax is shared between buyers and sellers. This depends on the relative price elasticities of supply and demand—NOT on who legally pays the tax to the government!
⚠️ The Critical Insight: It Doesn't Matter Who Pays the Tax! The final prices and quantity are identical regardless of whether the tax is collected from buyers or sellers. The statutory incidence (who writes the check) is NOT the same as the economic incidence (who actually bears the burden).
Tax Wedge: PB − PS = Tax (t)

📈 Graph: Tax on Buyers vs. Tax on Sellers

Key Insight: The final outcome—Q₁, PB, and PS—is identical regardless of who pays the tax. Who writes the check doesn't matter for the economic outcome.

🧮 Key Formulas

Tax = PB − PS
Government Revenue = Tax × Q₁
DWL = ½ × (Q₀ − Q₁) × (PB − PS)
Subsidy Cost = Subsidy per unit × Q₁
CS = ½ × Q* × (Pmax − P*)
PS = ½ × Q* × (P* − Pmin)

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Rent Control

Scenario: The city government imposes a price ceiling on rental apartments at PR, below the equilibrium price PE. Analyze the welfare effects.

Solution: The new quantity is QR (quantity supplied at PR). ΔCS = D − C (ambiguous). ΔPS = −(D + E). DWL = C + E.

Winners: Consumers who can still rent apartments (they get lower rent) — gain area D.
Losers: Landlords (get lower rent and rent fewer units) — lose D + E.
Also Losers: Consumers who can no longer find apartments — lose area C.

Scenario 2: Minimum Wage

Scenario: The government imposes a minimum wage at Wmin above the equilibrium wage WE. Analyze the welfare effects.

Solution: The new quantity is QD (quantity demanded at Wmin). ΔCS (Firms) = −(B + C). ΔPS (Workers) = B − F (ambiguous). DWL = C + F.

Winners: Workers who keep their jobs and get higher wages — gain area B (transfer from firms).
Losers: Firms (pay higher wages and hire fewer workers) — lose B + C.
Also Losers: Workers who lose their jobs — lose area F.

Scenario 3: Tax Incidence Calculation

Scenario: In the market for coffee, QD = 200 − 10P and QS = −40 + 50P. The government imposes a unit tax of t = 6. Find the new equilibrium and tax incidence.

Solution: Initial equilibrium: P = 4, Q = 160. With tax: PB = 9, PS = 3, Qt = 110. Consumers pay 5 more (burden of 5/6 of the tax). Producers receive 1 less (burden of 1/6 of the tax). Consumers bear most of the burden (≈83%).

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Price Ceilings and Floors: Price ceiling = maximum price → set BELOW equilibrium → creates SHORTAGE. Price floor = minimum price → set ABOVE equilibrium → creates SURPLUS.
  • Forgetting the Short Side of the Market: When there's a shortage or surplus, the quantity traded is the LOWER of the two quantities.
  • Thinking Deadweight Loss Includes Tax Revenue: Tax revenue is a transfer; DWL is the loss that benefits no one.
  • Ignoring Long-Run Effects: Rent Control: Shortage gets WORSE in long run. Minimum Wage: Unemployment gets WORSE in long run.
  • Assuming Who Pays the Tax Matters: Statutory incidence ≠ Economic incidence. The side with the more inelastic demand/supply bears more of the burden.

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: A price ceiling set below equilibrium creates:
✅ Correct Answer: B
A price ceiling set below equilibrium creates a shortage (excess demand) because quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
Q2: A price floor set above equilibrium creates:
✅ Correct Answer: B
A price floor set above equilibrium creates a surplus (excess supply) because quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.
Q3: In a shortage, the quantity traded is determined by:
✅ Correct Answer: B
In a shortage, the short side of the market prevails. Since sellers control how much they supply, the quantity traded is QS.
Q4: A tax is imposed on a good. Buyers pay $12 and sellers receive $8. What is the tax per unit?
✅ Correct Answer: A
Tax = PB − PS = $12 − $8 = $4
Q5: The more inelastic side of the market bears:
✅ Correct Answer: B
According to the Golden Rule of Tax Incidence, the burden falls most heavily on the side of the market that is MORE INELASTIC.

📖 Glossary

Price Ceiling
A legal maximum price that can be charged. Binding if set below equilibrium.
Price Floor
A legal minimum price that can be charged. Binding if set above equilibrium.
Binding
When a price control actually affects the market.
Shortage (Excess Demand)
Occurs when quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
Surplus (Excess Supply)
Occurs when quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.
Short Side of the Market
The side with the smaller quantity; determines quantity traded in disequilibrium.
Rent Control
A price ceiling on rental housing.
Minimum Wage
A price floor on labor (wages).
Monopsony
A market with only one buyer (market power on the demand side).
Direct Tax
Tax levied on individual income/wealth.
Indirect Tax
Tax levied on market transactions.
Tax Wedge
The gap between what buyers pay and what sellers receive (PB − PS).
Tax Incidence
How the burden of a tax is shared between buyers and sellers.
Statutory Incidence
Who is legally required to pay the tax.
Economic Incidence
Who actually bears the burden of the tax.
Deadweight Loss (DWL)
The net loss in total surplus caused by a policy.
Subsidy
A government payment that lowers the effective cost of production/consumption.
Natural Experiment
An empirical study comparing a group affected by a policy change to a similar unaffected group.
