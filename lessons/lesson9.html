🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to the world of market structures! So far in microeconomics, you've explored two extremes: Perfect Competition (many tiny firms, no market power) and Pure Monopoly (one giant firm, total market power). But most real-world markets fall somewhere in between. This chapter introduces Oligopoly – a market dominated by a few large firms – and explains why their strategic interactions are fascinating and economically important.

Why should you care? Because oligopolies are everywhere! Think about:
  • Airlines (Swiss, Air France, Lufthansa)
  • Mobile phone providers (Swisscom, Sunrise, Salt)
  • Supermarkets (Migros, Coop, Aldi)
  • Soft drinks (Coca-Cola vs. Pepsi)
Understanding oligopoly explains why prices aren't always as low as they could be, why firms advertise so heavily, and why governments have laws against "collusion" (firms secretly agreeing to keep prices high).

🔹 What is an Oligopoly?

Definition: An oligopoly is a market structure with a small number of large firms. Each firm has significant market power (it can influence price), but unlike a monopoly, firms must consider how their competitors will react to their decisions.

Real-World Example: Imagine the market for smartphones. Apple, Samsung, and Google (with a few others) dominate. If Apple decides to increase prices, they need to think: "Will Samsung keep prices the same (allowing Apple to make more profit), or will Samsung lower prices to steal our customers?" This mutual awareness is the essence of oligopoly.

Why do oligopolies exist? Because of barriers to entry – factors that make it difficult for new firms to enter the market:

  • High start-up costs (building a new airline requires billions for planes and airports)
  • Patents and intellectual property (pharmaceutical companies)
  • Economies of scale (it's cheaper per unit to produce a lot, so small new firms can't compete on cost)
  • Brand loyalty (people trust Coca-Cola – a new cola brand would struggle)

Because of these barriers, existing firms can earn positive economic profits (π > 0) in the long run, unlike in perfect competition where profits are competed away.

The Residual Demand Function

A firm in an oligopoly doesn't face the entire market demand curve. Instead, it faces the residual demand – what's left over after competitors have supplied their output.

Residual Demand = Market Demand − Quantity Supplied by Competitors
Example: If total market demand is 100 units and competitors supply 60 units, your residual demand is 40 units. If you want to sell 10 more units, you must reduce price, and competitors might react.

Conflicting Incentives – The Core Trade-off

This is THE most important idea in oligopoly:

Long-Term IncentiveShort-Term Incentive
ActionCooperate/ColludeDeviate/Cheat
Why?Restrict output → higher prices → higher industry profitsIncrease your output → capture larger market share → higher individual profit... briefly.
RiskOther firms might cheat (then you lose out)Other firms will retaliate (then everyone loses)

This tension is why oligopoly is so interesting – and why game theory is the perfect tool to analyze it.

🔹 The Duopoly Numerical Example

Definition: A duopoly is an oligopoly with exactly two firms. We'll analyze this case because the math is simple and the insights extend to larger oligopolies.

The Setup

  • Two identical firms: Firm 1 and Firm 2
  • They produce the same good (homogeneous product)
  • Market (inverse) demand: P = 13 − Q, where Q = Q₁ + Q₂
  • Both firms have MC = 1, FC = 0

Step 1: The Two Extreme Benchmarks

StructureConditionCalculationQPπ
Perfect CompetitionP = MC13 − Q = 1 → Q = 121210
MonopolyMR = MCMR = 13 − 2Q; 13 − 2Q = 1 → Q = 66736

Interpretation: Perfect competition produces the most output at the lowest price. Monopoly produces the least output at the highest price. Oligopoly will fall somewhere in between.

Step 2: The Collusive Solution (Cooperation)

If the two firms agree to act like a monopoly and split everything equally:

  • They produce Q = 6 total (the monopoly quantity)
  • Each produces Q₁ = Q₂ = 3
  • Price: P = 13 − 6 = 7
  • Industry profit = 36 (same as monopoly)
  • Each firm's profit = 18
⚠️ Key Insight: This is the "dream outcome" for the firms – maximum collective profit. But is it stable?

Step 3: The Incentive to Deviate (Cheating)

Suppose Firm 2 sticks to the agreement and produces Q₂ = 3.

  • Firm 1's profit function: π₁ = (13 − Q₁ − Q₂) × Q₁ − 1 × Q₁
  • With Q₂ = 3: π₁ = (10 − Q₁) × Q₁ − Q₁ = 9Q₁ − Q₁²
  • Maximize: dπ₁/dQ₁ = 9 − 2Q₁ = 0 → Q₁ = 4.5

What happens?

  • Total quantity: Q = 4.5 + 3 = 7.5
  • New price: P = 13 − 7.5 = 5.5
  • Firm 1's profit: π₁ = (5.5 − 1) × 4.5 = 20.25 (> 18 – better for cheater!)
  • Firm 2's profit: π₂ = (5.5 − 1) × 3 = 13.5 (< 18 – worse for the sucker)
Moral of the story: In the short term, cheating pays! The deviator increases profit from 18 to 20.25. But the other firm gets hurt (13.5) and will retaliate in the next period.

Step 4: The Non-Cooperative Equilibrium (Cournot-Nash Result)

Now both firms decide simultaneously, each maximizing their own profit while taking the other's quantity as given.

  • Firm 1's profit: π₁ = 12Q₁ − Q₁² − Q₂Q₁
  • Firm 2's profit: π₂ = 12Q₂ − Q₂² − Q₁Q₂
  • First-Order Conditions: Q₁ = (12 − Q₂)/2, Q₂ = (12 − Q₁)/2
  • Solve simultaneously: Q₁ = 4, Q₂ = 4

What happens?

  • Total quantity: Q = 8
  • Price: P = 13 − 8 = 5
  • Each firm's profit: π = (5 − 1) × 4 = 16
⚠️ Key Insight: Neither firm can improve its profit by unilaterally changing quantity. This is a Nash equilibrium.

Step 5: Summary of the Three Outcomes

🔹 Introduction to Game Theory

Definition: Game theory is the study of strategic decision-making – analyzing situations where the outcome depends on what multiple "players" do, and each player knows this.

Why do we need game theory? Oligopolies are strategic environments. A firm can't just say, "We'll produce X units." It must think, "If we produce X, how will our competitors respond?" Game theory provides the mathematical framework for analyzing these situations.

The Three Essential Components of a Game

  • Players: The decision-makers (e.g., Firm 1, Firm 2; Air France, Swiss)
  • Strategies: All possible actions each player can take (e.g., "Produce 48 flights" or "Produce 64 flights")
  • Payoffs: The outcomes (profits, years in prison, etc.) for every possible combination of strategies

The Payoff Matrix

A convenient table showing payoffs for each combination of strategies. By convention:

  • First number = payoff for Row player (player on the left)
  • Second number = payoff for Column player (player on top)
The Air France vs. Swiss Example:

Two airlines deciding how many flights between Zurich and Paris (48 or 64).

Air France: 48Air France: 64
Swiss: 48(46, 46)(38, 51)
Swiss: 64(51, 38)(41, 41)

Finding the Dominant Strategy

For Swiss (row player):

  • If AF chooses 48 → Swiss gets 46 (if 48) or 51 (if 64) → 64 is better
  • If AF chooses 64 → Swiss gets 38 (if 48) or 41 (if 64) → 64 is better
  • Conclusion: 64 is Swiss's dominant strategy

For Air France (column player):

  • If Swiss chooses 48 → AF gets 46 (if 48) or 51 (if 64) → 64 is better
  • If Swiss chooses 64 → AF gets 38 (if 48) or 41 (if 64) → 64 is better
  • Conclusion: 64 is Air France's dominant strategy
Dominant Strategy: A strategy that yields the highest payoff for a player regardless of what the other player does.

Nash Equilibrium

The outcome (64, 64) is a Nash equilibrium because:

  • Given Air France chooses 64, Swiss does best by choosing 64
  • Given Swiss chooses 64, Air France does best by choosing 64
  • No one can unilaterally improve their payoff by changing strategy
Nash Equilibrium: A set of strategies where no player can improve their outcome by unilaterally changing their strategy, given what the other players are doing.

🔹 The Prisoner's Dilemma

Definition: A classic game showing why rational individuals often fail to cooperate even when cooperation would make them both better off.

The Story: Two suspects are arrested for a crime. The police lack sufficient evidence for the main charge but have enough for a minor charge.
Prisoner 2: ConfessPrisoner 2: Not Confess
Prisoner 1: Confess(8 years, 8 years)(0 years, 20 years)
Prisoner 1: Not Confess(20 years, 0 years)(1 year, 1 year)

The Analysis

For Prisoner 1:

  • If P2 confesses → confess gives 8 years, not confess gives 20 years → confess is better
  • If P2 doesn't confess → confess gives 0 years, not confess gives 1 year → confess is better
  • Confess is the dominant strategy

For Prisoner 2: Same logic → Confess is the dominant strategy

Nash Equilibrium: (Confess, Confess) → 8 years each

The Tragedy

  • Pareto Optimal outcome: (Not Confess, Not Confess) → 1 year each
  • But this is NOT a Nash equilibrium because each prisoner has an incentive to cheat (confess)
Real-World Parallel: The prisoner's dilemma explains why oligopolists often fail to cooperate even though cooperation would increase their profits. They're afraid the other firm will cheat!

🔹 Externalities

Definition: An externality occurs when someone's actions affect another person's well-being without compensation or reward. It's an "uncompensated impact."

Why do externalities matter? Externalities are a type of market failure – the market price doesn't reflect the full social cost or benefit of a good. This leads to overproduction (negative externalities) or underproduction (positive externalities) from society's perspective.

TypeDefinitionExample
Negative Production ExternalityA firm's production imposes costs on others without payingFactory polluting a river, truck emitting black smoke
Negative Consumption ExternalityA person's consumption imposes costs on othersSmoking in a public place, driving during rush hour
Positive Production ExternalityA firm's production benefits others without rewardA company's R&D that creates knowledge others can use
Positive Consumption ExternalityA person's consumption benefits others without rewardGetting a flu vaccination, cycling to reduce traffic and pollution
Professor's Anecdotes (Real-Life Examples):
  • Negative (Production): The professor got stuck behind a truck emitting black smoke – the truck driver didn't pay for the health costs imposed.
  • Negative (Consumption): The professor (hypothetically) scared an elderly pedestrian by cycling on the footpath – no compensation was paid.
  • Positive (Consumption): Cycling to work is healthier and reduces noise; the cyclist isn't rewarded for these benefits.

Why Externalities Are "Market Failures"

  • Negative externality: The actor doesn't pay for the cost they impose → they produce/consume too much
  • Positive externality: The actor isn't rewarded for the benefit they provide → they produce/consume too little
⚠️ Key Insight: Externalities explain why governments intervene in markets – to correct these inefficiencies.

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Analyzing the Incentive to Cheat

Scenario: Two firms agree to collude and split monopoly profit equally. Alpha is considering whether to cheat. What happens?

Solution: Alpha's profit increases from 18 to 20.25 by cheating. Beta's profit falls from 18 to 13.5. In the short term, cheating pays! But Beta will retaliate in the future.

Scenario 2: Finding the Non-Cooperative Equilibrium

Scenario: P = 13 − (Q_A + Q_B), MC = 1. Find the Cournot-Nash equilibrium.

Solution: Q_A = (12 − Q_B)/2, Q_B = (12 − Q_A)/2. Solve: Q_A = 4, Q_B = 4. P = 5. Each profit = 16.

Scenario 3: The Prisoner's Dilemma Logic

Scenario: Two firms choose "Advertise" or "Don't Advertise." Payoffs: both don't advertise = (100, 100); both advertise = (60, 60); one advertises = (120, 40). What happens?

Solution: "Advertise" is the dominant strategy for both. Nash Equilibrium: (Advertise, Advertise) → (60, 60). Both would prefer (Don't, Don't) → (100, 100), but each has an incentive to cheat.

Scenario 4: Multiple Equilibria (Arms Race)

Scenario: USA and USSR choose "Arm" or "Disarm." Payoffs: (Disarm, Disarm) = (4, 4); (Arm, Arm) = (3, 3); (Arm, Disarm) = (3, 1); (Disarm, Arm) = (1, 3).

Solution: Two Nash equilibria: (Disarm, Disarm) and (Arm, Arm). (Disarm, Disarm) is Pareto optimal (better for both), but both are Nash equilibria.

Scenario 5: Cartels and Oligopoly

Scenario: Which statement is correct about cartels?

Solution: Creating a cartel is optimal for firms (maximizes joint profits), but cartels are unstable because each firm has an incentive to cheat. Cartel quantity = monopoly quantity < competitive quantity. Consumer surplus is the same as monopoly.

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Market Demand vs. Residual Demand: A firm in an oligopoly faces residual demand, not the entire market demand curve.
  • Thinking Collusion is Stable: The collusive solution is NOT a Nash equilibrium because each firm has an incentive to cheat.
  • Confusing Pareto Optimal with Equilibrium: A Pareto optimal outcome might NOT be a Nash equilibrium.
  • Forgetting Antitrust Laws: Explicit collusion (cartels) is illegal in most countries.
  • Mixing Up Profits Across Scenarios: Collusion: π = 18 each; Cheating: Cheater π = 20.25, Victim π = 13.5; Non-cooperative: π = 16 each.

Collusion
When firms secretly cooperate to maximize joint profits.
Cartel

Deviation / Cheating

Game Theory

Players
The decision-makers in a game.
Strategies
The possible actions available to each player.
Payoffs

A situation where no player can improve by unilaterally changing strategy.
Pareto Optimal

Externality
