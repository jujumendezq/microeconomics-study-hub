🏪 7. Supply in Competitive Markets

Firm behavior, profit maximization, and supply curves

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what it means for a firm to be a "price taker" and why this matters
  • Identify the goal of a firm: profit maximization
  • Calculate the profit-maximizing quantity using total revenue, total cost, marginal revenue, and marginal cost
  • Derive the supply curve of a competitive firm from its marginal cost curve
  • Distinguish between the short-run shutdown decision and the long-run exit decision
  • Explain the difference between a firm's supply curve and the market/sector supply curve
  • Understand how free entry leads to zero economic profits in the long run
  • Apply the concepts of break-even price and shutdown price
  • Differentiate between short-run and long-run supply curves
  • Explain why firms might stay in business despite making losses

🔹 Topic Introduction

Imagine you own a small bakery. Every day, you must decide: How many loaves of bread should I bake? If the market price of bread is high, you want to bake more. If the price drops, you might bake fewer loaves. But what about your rent? You have to pay that regardless of how much you bake. And what if the price drops so low that you can't even cover your ingredients? Should you keep baking or close the bakery temporarily?

This chapter answers these questions by examining how individual firms make production decisions. The goal is to understand the supply side of markets—how firms decide what quantity to produce, when to stay in business, and when to exit. This is the foundation for understanding market outcomes, prices, and the supply curves you see in economics textbooks.

Why should you care? This isn't just theory—it explains real business decisions. Why did some restaurants survive COVID while others closed? Why do gas stations raise prices when oil prices increase? Why does your favorite smartphone become cheaper over time? All of these questions connect to the concepts in this chapter.

🔹 Perfect Competition

Definition: Perfect competition is a market structure with five key characteristics:

  1. Atomicity: Many buyers and sellers, each too small to influence the market price
  2. Homogeneous goods: All firms sell identical products
  3. Perfect information: Everyone knows about prices, quality, and technology
  4. Free entry and exit: Firms can enter or leave the market without barriers
  5. Price takers: Individual firms cannot influence the market price—they must accept the price determined by market supply and demand
Real-World Example: Think about wheat farming. There are thousands of wheat farmers worldwide. Each farmer's output is a tiny fraction of total wheat production. All wheat is essentially the same. Farmers can easily stop growing wheat and start growing corn (free exit), and new farmers can enter. No single farmer can charge more than the market price—customers would just buy from another farmer. Farmers are price takers.

Why are firms price takers? If one firm tries to raise its price above the market price, it loses all its customers (they'll buy from competitors at the lower market price). Conversely, there's no reason to charge less than the market price because the firm can sell all it wants at the current market price. This creates a perfectly elastic (horizontal) demand curve facing the individual firm.

⚠️ Key Insight: Perfect competition is the "baseline" market structure that economists use as a benchmark. It's the starting point before studying more realistic markets like monopoly (one seller) or oligopoly (few sellers).

🔹 Profit Maximization

Definition: Profit maximization is the firm's primary objective. Firms choose the quantity of output (q) that maximizes the difference between total revenue (TR) and total cost (TC).

Profit (π) = Total Revenue (TR) - Total Cost (TC)

Why do firms maximize profit? Firms exist to make money for their owners. To maximize profits, a firm must compare:

  • What it earns from producing one more unit (marginal revenue, MR)
  • What it costs to produce that one more unit (marginal cost, MC)
Decision Rules:
  • If MR > MC, producing the extra unit adds to profit → Increase production
  • If MR < MC, the extra unit reduces profit → Decrease production
  • If MR = MC, profit is maximized → This is the optimal quantity
TermDefinitionFormula
Total Revenue (TR)Total money from selling outputTR = P × q
Total Cost (TC)Total cost of producing outputTC = FC + VC(q)
Profit (π)Revenue minus costπ = TR − TC
Marginal Revenue (MR)Additional revenue from one more unitMR = ΔTR/Δq
Marginal Cost (MC)Additional cost of one more unitMC = ΔTC/Δq

🔹 Marginal Revenue (MR) in Perfect Competition

Definition: Marginal revenue is the additional revenue a firm earns from selling one more unit of output. In perfect competition, MR = P (the market price). This is because the firm can sell any quantity at the same price.

Real-World Example: If the market price of wheat is $5 per bushel, then selling one extra bushel gives the firm exactly $5 more revenue. It doesn't have to lower its price to sell more because the firm is too small to affect the market price.

Why is MR = P in perfect competition?

TR = P × q (since P is constant)
MR = ΔTR/Δq = Δ(P × q)/Δq = P

So MR equals the price and doesn't change as q increases—it's a horizontal line at the market price.

⚠️ Key Distinction: MR = P only under perfect competition (price takers). For monopoly or firms with market power, MR < P.

🔹 Marginal Cost (MC)

Definition: Marginal cost is the additional cost incurred from producing one more unit of output.

MC = ΔTC/Δq = ΔVC/Δq (FC doesn't change with q, so MC depends only on variable costs)
Real-World Example: A bakery producing 100 loaves of bread per day has total costs of $200. If it increases production to 101 loaves and total costs rise to $203, the marginal cost of the 101st loaf is $3.

Why is MC typically U-shaped?

  • Decreasing phase (slope ↓): Initially, as output increases, workers become more productive (increasing marginal product). Additional units cost less to produce.
  • Increasing phase (slope ↑): Eventually, diminishing returns set in—each additional worker adds less to output, so the cost of producing each extra unit rises.

⚠️ Key Insight: MC is the foundation for the firm's supply curve. The profit-maximizing condition (P = MC) means the MC curve is the supply curve (above the shutdown price).

🔹 Average Cost (AC) and Average Variable Cost (AVC)

Average Cost (AC) = Total cost divided by quantity

AC = TC/q = FC/q + VC/q

Average Variable Cost (AVC) = Variable cost divided by quantity

AVC = VC/q
Real-World Example: If your bakery has fixed costs of $100/day (rent) and variable costs of $200/day (ingredients, wages) to produce 100 loaves:
TC = $100 + $200 = $300
AC = $300/100 = $3 per loaf
AVC = $200/100 = $2 per loaf
AFC = $100/100 = $1 per loaf

Why does AC decline initially?

  • AC declines initially because fixed costs are spread over more units (AFC falls)
  • AC eventually rises when MC exceeds AC, pulling the average up
  • AVC is always below AC because AC includes both variable and fixed costs
📌 The Critical Relationship: MC intersects AC at AC's minimum.
  • If MC < AC, producing one more unit pulls AC down
  • If MC > AC, producing one more unit pulls AC up
  • At MC = AC, AC is at its minimum point

🔹 The Profit-Maximizing Condition

Definition: The firm maximizes profit by producing the quantity where:

MR = MC

In perfect competition, since MR = P:

P = MC(q*)

Where q* is the profit-maximizing quantity.

Real-World Example: Using the bakery example with market price P = $171:
  • MC of 2nd loaf = $90 → MR ($171) > MC ($90) → Produce it!
  • MC of 3rd loaf = $170 → MR ($171) > MC ($170) → Produce it!
  • MC of 4th loaf = $190 → MR ($171) < MC ($190) → Stop at 3

The firm produces 3 loaves—the largest quantity where MR ≥ MC.

⚠️ Discrete-Goods Caveat: When producing whole units, choose the largest quantity where MR ≥ MC (the smallest positive difference).

🔹 The Firm's Supply Curve

Definition: The firm's supply curve shows the quantity the firm is willing to produce at each market price. For a competitive firm, the supply curve is the portion of the MC curve above the minimum AVC (short run) or above minimum AC (long run).

At any price P ≥ minimum AVC, firm supplies q where P = MC(q)
At any price P < minimum AVC, firm supplies q = 0 (shuts down)
Real-World Example: If the bakery's MC curve shows:
  • At P = $91, it produces q = 2 (where MC ≈ $91)
  • At P = $171, it produces q = 3 (where MC = $170)
  • At P = $250, it produces q = 4 (where MC ≈ $240)

The supply curve is upward-sloping because higher prices induce the firm to produce more.

Why is the supply curve MC above minimum AVC?

  • If price is below AVC, the firm can't even cover its variable costs
  • It would lose less money by shutting down and only paying fixed costs than by continuing to produce

🔹 Shutdown vs. Exit

This is one of the most important distinctions in this chapter!

Shutdown (Short-Run Decision)

  • Temporary decision to stop production
  • Firm pays fixed costs but avoids variable costs
  • Happens when P < minimum AVC
  • Firm expects to reopen when conditions improve

Exit (Long-Run Decision)

  • Permanent decision to leave the market
  • Firm eliminates both fixed and variable costs
  • Happens when P < minimum AC
  • Firm permanently stops all operations
Real-World Example:
  • Shutdown: An ice cream shop closes for winter. It still pays rent and insurance (fixed costs) but doesn't pay for ice cream, toppings, or seasonal workers (variable costs). It reopens in spring.
  • Exit: The ice cream shop closes permanently. It sells its equipment, terminates its lease, and goes out of business.

Produce (profit)BothP > ACPay all costs, make profit

⚠️ Key Insight: This distinction explains why firms sometimes stay in business despite making losses (short-run: price covers variable costs but not fixed costs) but eventually exit if losses persist (long-run: price doesn't cover total costs).

🔹 Break-Even Price and Shutdown Price

Break-Even Price (BE)

= Minimum of Average Cost (AC)

  • Price where P = AC, so profit = 0
  • At this price, the firm earns zero economic profit
  • Below BE price → firm exits in the long run

Shutdown Price (SD)

= Minimum of Average Variable Cost (AVC)

  • Price where P = AVC
  • At this price, the firm is indifferent between producing and shutting down
  • Below SD price → firm shuts down in the short run
Real-World Example:
  • Bakery: AC minimum = $150 per loaf → break-even price = $150
  • At $150/loaf: zero economic profit
  • At $120/loaf: losses → may exit if persistent
  • Bakery: AVC minimum = $80 per loaf → shutdown price = $80
  • At $80/loaf: indifferent to producing
  • At $70/loaf: can't cover variable costs → shut down

🔹 Market/Sector Supply Curve

Definition: The market supply curve is the horizontal sum of all individual firms' supply curves. It shows the total quantity supplied by all firms at each market price.

Market Quantity (Q) = Sum of all firms' quantities (q₁ + q₂ + q₃ + ...)
Real-World Example: If there are 100 identical bakeries and each supplies 3 loaves at P = $171, the market supply at P = $171 is 300 loaves. At P = $200, if each supplies 4 loaves, market supply is 400 loaves.

In the short run: Number of firms (N) is fixed. Each firm adjusts quantity along its MC curve (intensive margin). Market supply = sum of individual MC curves.

In the long run: Number of firms adjusts through entry and exit (extensive margin). Free entry: if firms earn profits (P > AC), new firms enter. Free exit: if firms earn losses (P < AC), firms exit. Long-run equilibrium: P = min AC and economic profits = 0.

🔹 Free Entry and Zero Profits

In a perfectly competitive market with free entry, the long-run equilibrium is characterized by:

P = minimum AC
Economic profit = 0

This is the "zero-profit condition."

Real-World Example: Think about the restaurant industry. When restaurants are profitable, new ones open. When too many open, competition drives prices down. In the long run, restaurants earn just enough to cover costs—not huge profits, but a "normal" return on investment. This is why many restaurants seem to barely break even.

Why does this happen?

  • If P > AC, firms earn positive profits → positive profits attract new firms (free entry) → new firms increase market supply (S shifts right) → market price falls → process continues until profits are zero (P = AC)
  • If P < AC, firms earn losses → losses cause firms to exit → exit decreases market supply (S shifts left) → market price rises → process continues until losses are eliminated (P = AC)
⚠️ Key Insight: Zero profits in economics does not mean zero accounting profits. Economic profit includes opportunity costs. "Normal profit" is the return the owner could earn elsewhere—it's included in AC. So zero economic profit means the firm is earning a competitive return, just not "above normal."

🔹 Economic vs. Accounting Profits

Accounting Profit: Total revenue minus explicit costs (monetary outflows like wages, rent, materials).

Economic Profit: Total revenue minus both explicit and implicit costs (opportunity costs, like the owner's foregone salary or the foregone return on invested capital).

Accounting Profit = TR − Explicit Costs
Economic Profit = TR − (Explicit Costs + Implicit Costs)
Real-World Example: You own a bakery:
  • Total revenue: $100,000
  • Explicit costs (wages, ingredients, rent): $70,000
  • Accounting profit: $30,000
  • Implicit costs: You could earn $40,000 as a manager elsewhere; you could earn $5,000 interest on your $100,000 investment
  • Total implicit cost: $45,000
  • Economic profit: $100,000 − $70,000 − $45,000 = −$15,000

Conclusion: You're making an accounting profit ($30,000) but an economic loss ($15,000). You'd be better off closing the bakery and working as a manager.

⚠️ Key Insight: "Zero economic profit" means the firm is earning a normal return on investment. The owner is compensated for their time and capital, just not earning anything above what they could earn elsewhere.

🔹 Why Firms Stay in Business Despite Losses (Sunk Costs)

Firms may continue operating in the short run even when making losses because fixed costs are sunk costs—they cannot be recovered if the firm shuts down or exits.

Real-World Example: Consider a theater company during COVID-19. They had to cancel all performances, but still had to pay rent on the theater and salaries for permanent staff (fixed costs). By producing online content (even if it lost money), they at least covered some variable costs. Shutting down completely would have meant losing all the fixed costs anyway—so staying open was the better choice.

The Shutdown Decision Rule (Short Run):

The firm compares two options:

  • Produce: π(q) = TR − TC = P×q − VC(q) − FC
  • Shut down: π(0) = −FC

The firm produces if:

π(q) ≥ π(0)
P×q − VC(q) − FC ≥ −FC
P×q ≥ VC(q)
P ≥ AVC(q)
Key Insight: As long as P ≥ AVC (the price covers variable costs), the firm loses less by producing than by shutting down. The fixed costs are sunk—they're gone regardless.

Real-World Reasons Firms Stay Despite Losses

  • Temporary price fluctuations: Firms believe prices will recover soon
  • Sunk costs: Many costs (buildings, equipment, licenses) cannot be recovered
  • Strategic reasons: Maintaining market presence, reputation, and customer relationships
  • Hope for future: Firms are "betting" on future price increases
  • Government shutdowns: During forced closures, firms keep their "doors open" (remain active) even if production is temporarily zero
The "Skinny Cow, Fat Cow" Analogy: Firms think in terms of cycles. They might lose money during "skinny cow" years (low prices) but stay in business because they expect "fat cow" years (high prices) in the future. This is like a farmer who keeps cows even during a drought, knowing that better times will come.

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Profit Maximization

Scenario: A competitive firm faces P = 50. TC = 100 + 10q + 2q². Find the profit-maximizing quantity and profit.

Solution: MC = 10 + 4q. Set P = MC: 50 = 10 + 4q → q* = 10. TR = 500. TC = 100 + 100 + 200 = 400. π = 500 − 400 = 100.

Scenario 2: Shutdown Decision

Scenario: A firm has FC = 50, VC = 20q + 2q², P = 30. Should the firm shut down? What is the shutdown price?

Solution: AVC = 20 + 2q. Minimum AVC = 20. P = 30 > 20 → Do NOT shut down. Profit-maximizing q* = 2.5. π = −37.5 (loss). Shutdown loss = −50. Producing loses less → produce. Shutdown price = 20.

Scenario 3: Long-Run Exit

Scenario: Same firm, P = 15. Should the firm shut down? Exit?

Solution: P = 15 < AVC (20) → Shut down. AC minimum = 40. P = 15 < AC → Exit in the long run.

Scenario 4: Supply Curve Derivation

Scenario: MC = 2q + 4, minimum AVC = 4. What is the supply curve? At P = 10, what quantity?

Solution: Supply curve: q = (P − 4)/2 for P ≥ 4; q = 0 for P < 4. At P = 10: q = (10 − 4)/2 = 3.

Scenario 5: Market Supply and Entry

Scenario: 50 identical firms, each MC = 2q + 4, min AVC = 4. Market demand: Q = 500 − 10P. Find market supply and equilibrium.

Solution: Individual supply: q = (P − 4)/2. Market supply: Qs = 25P − 100. Equilibrium: 25P − 100 = 500 − 10P → P* ≈ 17.14, Q* ≈ 328.6.

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing "Shutdown" with "Exit": Shutdown is short-run (P < AVC). Exit is long-run (P < AC).
  • Confusing MR with Price: MR = P only in perfect competition. For firms with market power, MR < P.
  • Thinking Zero Profit Means Bankruptcy: Zero economic profit = normal return, not bankruptcy.
  • Forgetting the Supply Curve Starts at Minimum AVC: Below min AVC, the firm supplies zero.
  • Thinking Fixed Costs Affect MC: MC depends only on variable costs (ΔFC = 0).
