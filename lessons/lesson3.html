🔹 Topic Introduction

Elasticity is one of the most practical and powerful tools in microeconomics. While demand and supply curves tell us the direction of change when prices or other factors shift, elasticity tells us the magnitude—how much quantity will change. This matters for businesses deciding whether to raise prices, governments designing tax policies, and understanding who bears the burden of economic shocks.

Why should you care? If you ever run a business, set prices, or analyze markets, elasticity will help you predict consumer behavior and maximize revenue. It's also guaranteed to appear on your exam!

🔹 The Concept of Elasticity

Definition: Elasticity measures the sensitivity or responsiveness of one variable to changes in another variable. More formally, it's the percentage change in one variable resulting from a 1% increase in another variable.

Real-World Example: If the price of coffee increases by 10%, and as a result, the quantity demanded decreases by 20%, the demand for coffee is very sensitive to price changes—we say it's "elastic."

Elasticity vs. Slope: Why We Can't Just Use Slope

Problem 1: Units of Measurement

  • Suppose coffee demand: Price increases by $1, quantity falls by 10 tons
  • Suppose another good: Price increases by $1, quantity falls by 10 meters
  • How do we compare these? The units (tons vs. meters) are meaningless for comparison!

Problem 2: Scale Matters

  • A 10-ton drop is a lot if total demand is 20 tons (50% drop)
  • A 10-ton drop is tiny if total demand is 1,000 tons (1% drop)
  • Similarly, a $1 price increase is huge if the price is $2 (50% increase) but tiny if the price is $100 (1% increase)

The Solution: Elasticity uses percentages!

  • Percentages are unit-free, so we can compare across goods
  • Percentages account for the size of the change relative to the starting point
⚠️ Key Insight: Elasticity is related to slope but is not the same thing. A steep demand curve can be elastic or inelastic depending on where you are on the curve!
Elasticity = %Δ Variable A / %Δ Variable B = (ΔA/A) / (ΔB/B)
VariableDefinition
ΔAChange in variable A
AInitial value of variable A
ΔBChange in variable B
BInitial value of variable B

🔹 Own-Price Elasticity of Demand (εPD)

Definition: The percentage change in quantity demanded resulting from a 1% increase in the price of the good itself.

εPD = %ΔQD / %ΔP = (ΔQD/QD) / (ΔP/P)
εPD = (Q1D − Q0D) / Q0D ÷ (P1 − P0) / P0
VariableDefinition
Q0DInitial quantity demanded
Q1DNew quantity demanded
P0Initial price
P1New price

Step-by-Step Example

Scenario: The price of a pizza increases from $16 to $18. Quantity demanded falls from 300 pizzas to 240 pizzas.

  1. Identify values: P₀ = 16, P₁ = 18, Q₀ = 300, Q₁ = 240
  2. Calculate changes: ΔP = 2, ΔQ = −60
  3. Calculate percentage changes: %ΔP = 2/16 = 12.5%, %ΔQ = −60/300 = −20%
  4. Calculate elasticity: ε = −20% / 12.5% = −1.6
Interpretation: A 1% increase in price leads to a 1.6% decrease in quantity demanded. Demand is elastic.

Alternative Calculation Methods

MethodFormula
Initial Point (Standard)ε = (Q₁−Q₀)/Q₀ ÷ (P₁−P₀)/P₀
Final Pointε = (Q₁−Q₀)/Q₁ ÷ (P₁−P₀)/P₁
Midpoint (Arc)ε = (Q₁−Q₀)/((Q₀+Q₁)/2) ÷ (P₁−P₀)/((P₀+P₁)/2)
⚠️ Exam tip: If nothing is specified about which method to use, use the initial point method (Method 1).

Interpreting Elasticity Values

🔹 Elasticity Along a Linear Demand Curve

Key Insight: Even though a linear demand curve has a constant slope, the elasticity varies along the curve!

Why? Because elasticity = (1/slope) × (P/Q). Since P/Q changes as you move along the curve, elasticity changes too.

🔹 Price Elasticity of Demand and Total Revenue

Total Revenue (TR) = Price × Quantity = P × Q

The Critical Question: If you increase price, does total revenue increase or decrease?

If Demand Is...When Price IncreasesWhen Price Decreases
Elastic (|ε| > 1)Revenue DecreasesRevenue Increases
Unit-Elastic (|ε| = 1)Revenue UnchangedRevenue Unchanged
Inelastic (|ε| < 1)Revenue IncreasesRevenue Decreases

The Mathematics of Total Revenue and Elasticity

ΔTR/ΔP = Q(1 − |ε|)
If...Then...
|ε| < 1 (Inelastic)ΔTR/ΔP > 0 → Price ↑ → TR ↑
|ε| > 1 (Elastic)ΔTR/ΔP < 0 → Price ↑ → TR ↓
|ε| = 1 (Unit-Elastic)ΔTR/ΔP = 0 → Price ↑ → TR unchanged
Real-World Application: Agricultural Goods
A bad weather shock destroys crops → supply shifts LEFT → price increases, quantity decreases.
Demand for agricultural goods is typically inelastic (food is a necessity).
Result: Total revenue INCREASES despite the bad harvest!
This explains why farmers might support policies that restrict production.

🔹 Cross-Price Elasticity of Demand (εPyDx)

Definition: The percentage change in quantity demanded of good X resulting from a 1% increase in the price of good Y.

εPyDx = %ΔQxD / %ΔPy = (ΔQx/Qx) / (ΔPy/Py)

Interpretation

SignRelationshipExample
ε > 0SubstitutesPrice of Coke ↑ → Demand for Pepsi ↑
ε < 0ComplementsPrice of printers ↑ → Demand for ink ↓
ε ≈ 0UnrelatedComputers and grain
⚠️ IMPORTANT: We CANNOT take the absolute value here! Both sign AND magnitude matter.

Step-by-Step Example

Price of Coke increases from $2 to $2.20, demand for Pepsi increases from 100 to 120.

  • %ΔPCoke = 0.20/2 = 10%
  • %ΔQPepsi = 20/100 = 20%
  • ε = 20%/10% = 2.0
Interpretation: Coke and Pepsi are substitutes. A 1% increase in Coke price leads to a 2% increase in Pepsi demand.

🔹 Income Elasticity of Demand (εRD)

Definition: The percentage change in quantity demanded resulting from a 1% increase in income.

εRD = %ΔQD / %ΔR = (ΔQ/Q) / (ΔR/R)
SignClassificationDefinition
εRD > 0Normal GoodDemand increases when income increases
εRD < 0Inferior GoodDemand decreases when income increases
εRD > 1LuxuryDemand increases more than proportionally to income
0 < εRD < 1NecessityDemand increases less than proportionally to income

Step-by-Step Example

Income increases from $30,000 to $33,000, demand for restaurant meals increases from 50 to 60.

  • %ΔR = 3,000/30,000 = 10%
  • %ΔQ = 10/50 = 20%
  • εRD = 20%/10% = 2.0
Interpretation: Restaurant meals are a luxury normal good (ε > 1). A 1% increase in income leads to a 2% increase in demand.

Three Engel Laws

  • Law 1: Food expenditure share falls as income rises → Food is a normal necessity (0 < εRD < 1)
  • Law 2: Expenditure shares on clothing, lighting, heating, housing are independent of income → These goods have income elasticity near 1
  • Law 3: Expenditure shares on education, health, and leisure rise with income → These are normal luxury goods (εRD > 1)

🔹 Price Elasticity of Supply (εPS)

Definition: The percentage change in quantity supplied resulting from a 1% increase in price.

εPS = %ΔQS / %ΔP = (ΔQS/QS) / (ΔP/P)

Sign: ALWAYS POSITIVE (Law of Supply)

Special Cases

Elasticity ValueTermShapeWhat It Means
εPS = 0Perfectly InelasticVerticalQuantity doesn't change with price
εPS = ∞Perfectly ElasticHorizontalAny price below market → supply drops to zero
εPS = 1Unit-ElasticThrough originQuantity changes proportionally to price
εPS < 1Relatively InelasticSteepQuantity changes less than price
εPS > 1Relatively ElasticFlatQuantity changes more than price

Factors Affecting Price Elasticity of Supply

FactorExplanationExample
More inventoryCan sell from stock without new productionRetailers with large warehouses
Easier access to inputsCan hire workers/factors quicklyServices vs. specialized manufacturing
Longer time horizonMore time to adjust productionOil supply in 5 years vs. next month
Real-World Application: The 1973 Oil Crisis
OPEC embargo → Supply and demand for oil were both highly inelastic → World oil price quadrupled!
Why? Demand: Few substitutes for oil in the short run. Supply: Takes time to increase oil production.
Recent Changes: Fracking technology has made oil supply more elastic → Supply now adjusts more easily at around $70/barrel → Helps stabilize prices.

🔹 Consumer and Producer Surplus

Consumer Surplus (CS)

Definition: The difference between what buyers are willing to pay and what they actually pay.

CS = Willingness to Pay − Market Price
For linear demand: CS = ½ × Q* × (Pmax − P*)
Example: Demand: P = 50 − 2Q, Market price = $20.
Q* = 15, Pmax = 50
CS = ½ × 15 × (50 − 20) = 225

Producer Surplus (PS)

Definition: The difference between what sellers receive and what they're willing to accept.

PS = Market Price − Willingness to Sell
For linear supply: PS = ½ × Q* × (P* − Pmin)
Example: Supply: P = 5 + Q, Market price = $20.
Q* = 15, Pmin = 5
PS = ½ × 15 × (20 − 5) = 112.5
⚠️ Key Insight: Total Surplus (CS + PS) is maximized at market equilibrium.

🧮 All Formulas

Own-Price Elasticity: εPD = %ΔQD / %ΔP
Cross-Price Elasticity: εPyDx = %ΔQx / %ΔPy
Income Elasticity: εRD = %ΔQ / %ΔR
Price Elasticity of Supply: εPS = %ΔQS / %ΔP
Midpoint Method: ε = (Q₁−Q₀)/((Q₀+Q₁)/2) ÷ (P₁−P₀)/((P₀+P₁)/2)
Total Revenue: TR = P × Q
Consumer Surplus: CS = ½ × Q* × (Pmax − P*)
Producer Surplus: PS = ½ × Q* × (P* − Pmin)
Lerner Index: (P − MC) / P = 1 / |ε|

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Total Revenue and Agricultural Goods

Scenario: A bad weather shock destroys a significant portion of the wheat harvest. Using your knowledge of elasticity, analyze the effect on farmers' total revenue.

Solution: Demand for agricultural goods is typically inelastic (food is a necessity). When supply shifts LEFT, price increases a lot while quantity decreases only a little. Therefore, total revenue (P × Q) INCREASES despite the bad harvest.

Scenario 2: Olive Oil Price Increase

Scenario: You are a producer of olive oil considering increasing the price. Will your total revenue increase?

Solution: If olive oil has close substitutes (other cooking oils), demand may be elastic → price increase would REDUCE total revenue. If olive oil has few substitutes, demand may be inelastic → price increase would INCREASE total revenue.

Scenario 3: Energy Crisis

Scenario: Energy prices have risen sharply. Which type of household is hurt the most?

Solution: Energy is a necessity with low income elasticity. Low-income households spend a larger share of their income on energy. When energy prices rise, low-income households are hurt disproportionately.

Scenario 4: Drug Policy

Scenario: To fight drug-related crimes, authorities consider constraining supply or reducing demand. Which is more effective?

Solution: Drug demand is relatively inelastic (addiction), drug supply is relatively elastic. Constraining supply → Price increases a lot, quantity decreases only a little → Drug-related crime may INCREASE. Reducing demand → Quantity decreases a lot → Crime DECREASES. Reducing demand is more effective.

Scenario 5: Linear Demand Curve

Scenario: QD = 200 − 10P. At what price is demand unit-elastic?

Solution:

  • dQ/dP = −10
  • ε = −10 × (P/Q) = −10 × (P/(200−10P))
  • Set ε = −1: −10 × (P/(200−10P)) = −1
  • 10P = 200 − 10P → 20P = 200 → P = 10
Answer: At P = 10, demand is unit-elastic. Above P = 10, demand is elastic. Below P = 10, demand is inelastic.

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Elasticity with Slope: "A steep demand curve means demand is inelastic" — FALSE. Elasticity depends on both slope AND P/Q ratio.
  • Confusing Movement vs. Shift: "If income increases, we move along the demand curve" — FALSE. Income changes SHIFT the demand curve.
  • Incorrect Absolute Value for Cross-Price: "Cross-price elasticity is -0.5, so they are substitutes" — FALSE. Negative means COMPLEMENTS.
  • Mixing Up Classifications: "Normal goods have negative income elasticity" — FALSE. Normal goods have POSITIVE income elasticity.
  • Misapplying Revenue Rule: "If demand is elastic, increasing price increases revenue" — FALSE. Elastic demand → price ↑ → revenue ↓.
  • Forgetting the Law of Demand Sign: "Price elasticity of demand is positive" — FALSE. It's negative (we use absolute value for interpretation).
  • Confusing Luxury vs. Necessity: "Luxury goods have income elasticity between 0 and 1" — FALSE. Luxury goods have ε > 1.
  • Confusing Surplus: "Consumer surplus is the area above the demand curve" — FALSE. CS is BELOW demand and ABOVE market price.
  • The Reversibility Problem: "Elasticity from A to B is the same as from B to A" — FALSE. Use the midpoint method for consistent results.

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: The price of a good increases from $10 to $12, and quantity demanded falls from 100 to 80. What is the own-price elasticity of demand?
✅ Correct Answer: C
%ΔQ = (80-100)/100 = -20%
%ΔP = (12-10)/10 = 20%
ε = -20%/20% = -1.0
Q2: If demand is elastic (|ε| > 1), what happens to total revenue when price increases?
✅ Correct Answer: B
When demand is elastic, the quantity effect dominates. The percentage decrease in quantity is larger than the percentage increase in price, so total revenue decreases.
Q3: The price of Coke increases by 10%, and demand for Pepsi increases by 15%. What is the cross-price elasticity?
✅ Correct Answer: B
ε = 15%/10% = 1.5
Positive elasticity → Substitutes
Q4: Income increases by 5%, and demand for restaurant meals increases by 15%. What is the income elasticity?
✅ Correct Answer: B
εRD = 15%/5% = 3.0
Since ε > 1, restaurant meals are a luxury normal good
Q5: Given QD = 100 − 2P and QS = 20 + 2P, find equilibrium and consumer surplus.
✅ Correct Answer: A
100 − 2P = 20 + 2P → 80 = 4P → P* = 20
Q* = 60
Demand intercept: Q=0 → P=50
CS = ½ × 60 × (50 − 20) = 400

📖 Glossary

Elasticity
Measure of responsiveness of one variable to changes in another; the percentage change in one variable resulting from a 1% increase in another.
Own-price elasticity
Percentage change in quantity demanded from a 1% increase in the good's own price.
Cross-price elasticity
Percentage change in quantity demanded of one good from a 1% increase in the price of another good.
Income elasticity
Percentage change in quantity demanded from a 1% increase in income.
Price elasticity of supply
Percentage change in quantity supplied from a 1% increase in price.
Total Revenue
Price × Quantity; total amount received from selling a good.
Midpoint method
Method for calculating elasticity between two points that gives the same result regardless of direction.
Consumer Surplus
Difference between willingness to pay and actual price paid.
Producer Surplus
Difference between price received and willingness to sell.
Total Surplus
Sum of consumer and producer surplus; CS + PS.
Substitutes
Goods where an increase in the price of one leads to an increase in demand for the other.
Complements
Goods where an increase in the price of one leads to a decrease in demand for the other.
Normal Good
Demand increases when income increases (positive income elasticity).
Inferior Good
Demand decreases when income increases (negative income elasticity).
Luxury Good
Normal good where demand increases more than proportionally to income (εRD > 1).
Necessity Good
Normal good where demand increases less than proportionally to income (0 < εRD < 1).
