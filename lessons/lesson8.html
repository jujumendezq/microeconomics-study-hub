👑 8. Market Power: Monopoly

Single seller, price maker, and the welfare cost of market power

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Compare perfect competition vs. monopoly market structures
  • Identify the reasons why monopolies arise (entry barriers)
  • Explain why Marginal Revenue (MR) is lower than Price (P) for a monopolist
  • Determine the profit-maximizing quantity for a monopolist using MR = MC
  • Evaluate the welfare cost of monopoly (deadweight loss)
  • Identify situations where monopoly might be efficient (price discrimination, regulation, natural monopoly)
  • Understand the Lerner Index as a measure of market power
  • Explain price discrimination and its three types

🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to the world of market power! In previous chapters, you learned about perfect competition—a market where many small firms compete and no single firm can influence the market price. Now we're going to explore the complete opposite: monopoly. A monopoly exists when there is only one seller in the market for a good or service with no close substitutes. This single seller has enormous power over the price—they're a price maker, not a price taker.

Why should you care? Monopolies are everywhere in real life! Think about:
  • Your local utility company (electricity, water)
  • Pharmaceutical companies with patents on life-saving drugs
  • DeBeers in the diamond market
  • Your campus cafeteria (maybe not a pure monopoly, but close!)
Understanding monopoly helps you understand why some goods are expensive, why governments regulate certain industries, and how firms with market power make decisions.
⚠️ The Big Question: Is monopoly better or worse for society than perfect competition? The answer is ambiguous—it depends on the specific situation. Static welfare analysis says monopoly is worse, but dynamic considerations like innovation and natural monopolies can make monopoly preferable in some cases.

🔹 What is a Monopoly?

Definition: A monopoly is a market structure where a single seller produces a good or service with no close substitutes.

Simple Analogy: Imagine you're the only person in your town who sells drinking water. Everyone needs water, and they can only buy it from you. You have complete control over the price!

Key Characteristics of a Monopoly

  • Single Seller: One firm produces the entire market output
  • No Close Substitutes: Consumers can't easily switch to another product
  • Price Maker: The firm can influence market price by changing output
  • Barriers to Entry: Other firms cannot easily enter the market
  • Faces Entire Market Demand: The monopolist's demand curve IS the market demand curve
Real-World Examples:
  • DeBeers: Controlled ~40% of the world's diamond market
  • Local Utility Companies: Your electricity or water provider
  • Patent-Protected Drugs: Pharmaceutical companies with exclusive rights
  • SBB/CFF/FFS: Swiss Federal Railways (natural monopoly)

🔹 Why Do Monopolies Arise? (Barriers to Entry)

Definition: Barriers to entry are factors that prevent other firms from entering the market and competing with the monopolist.

Three Main Types of Barriers

Type 1: Legal Market Power (Government-Created)

The government gives a firm the exclusive right to produce a good or service.

  • Patents: Exclusive rights to produce an invention (e.g., pharmaceutical drugs)
  • Copyrights: Exclusive rights to creative works (e.g., books, music)
  • Licenses/Franchises: Government permission to operate (e.g., TV/radio stations)
  • Import Tariffs/Quotas: Restrictions on foreign competition
Why does the government do this? To encourage innovation! If companies know they'll have a temporary monopoly on their inventions, they're more willing to invest in expensive R&D. The trade-off: Political authorities accept the welfare loss of high prices to incentivize future creation.

Type 2: Exclusive Ownership of a Key Resource

A single firm owns a resource essential for production.

  • Alcoa: Controlled bauxite (used for aluminum production)
  • DeBeers: Controlled diamond mines (historically ~80% of supply)
  • A hotel that owns the only beachfront property on an island

Type 3: Natural Monopoly (Technical Monopoly)

When a single firm can produce at a lower cost than multiple firms.

  • Characteristics: Economies of Scale (increasing returns to scale), High Fixed Costs, low or zero Marginal Cost, AC > MC throughout the relevant output range
  • Examples: Electricity Distribution, Railway Networks (SBB/CFF/FFS in Switzerland), Bridges/Highways
⚠️ Key Insight: In a natural monopoly, breaking up the firm into competitors would increase average costs for everyone! This creates a trade-off between production efficiency and allocative efficiency.

🔹 Marginal Revenue in Monopoly (MR < P)

Definition: Marginal Revenue (MR) is the additional revenue a firm earns from selling one more unit of output.

The Big Difference from Perfect Competition:
  • In perfect competition: MR = P (firm can sell all it wants at the market price)
  • In monopoly: MR < P (to sell more, the firm must lower the price on ALL units)

Why is MR < P? (The Trade-Off)

When a monopolist wants to sell ONE more unit:

  • Good News 📈 The firm earns revenue on the additional unit sold
  • Bad News 📉 To sell that extra unit, the firm must lower the price; the lower price applies to ALL units sold
Example: Suppose a monopolist sells 4 units at $8 each:
  • Output 4 units: P = $8, TR = $32
  • Output 5 units: P = $7, TR = $35
  • MR = $35 − $32 = $3
  • Revenue from the 5th unit: $7 (good news)
  • Revenue lost by lowering price on first 4 units: $4 (bad news: $1 × 4 units)
  • Net MR = $7 − $4 = $3

MR is LESS than the price ($3 < $7)!

For linear demand: P = a − bQ → MR = a − 2bQ

The MR curve has the same intercept as demand but is twice as steep.

🔹 Profit Maximization for a Monopolist

The Rule: All firms (both competitive and monopoly) maximize profit where:

MR = MC

The Difference:

  • Perfect Competition: MR = P, so the rule becomes P = MC
  • Monopoly: MR < P, so the rule is MR = MC, but P > MC

Step-by-Step Reasoning

  • If MR > MC → Producing one more unit adds to profit → Do it!
  • If MR < MC → Producing one more unit reduces profit → Don't do it!
  • If MR = MC → Profit is maximized → Stop!
Numerical Example:
  • Demand: Q = 20 − P (or P = 20 − Q)
  • Total Cost: TC = 16 + 2Q
  • TR = P × Q = (20 − Q) × Q = 20Q − Q²
  • MR = dTR/dQ = 20 − 2Q
  • MC = dTC/dQ = 2
  • Set MR = MC: 20 − 2Q = 2 → Q = 9
  • P = 20 − 9 = 11
  • TR = 11 × 9 = 99, TC = 16 + 2(9) = 34
  • π = 99 − 34 = 65

Key Result: The monopolist produces where MR = MC, but charges a price above MC. At the optimum: P = 11, MC = 2. The markup (P − MC) = 9.

🔹 The Welfare Cost of Monopoly (Deadweight Loss)

Definition: Deadweight Loss (DWL) is the loss of total surplus (consumer surplus + producer surplus) that results from a market not being at the competitive equilibrium.

VariablePerfect CompetitionMonopoly
PriceLower (P = MC)Higher (P > MC)
QuantityHigher (QPC)Lower (QM)
Consumer SurplusLargerSmaller
Producer SurplusSmallerLarger
Total SurplusMaximizedReduced (DWL)
The Problem: When a monopolist reduces quantity from QPC to QM:
  • Some units that would have been produced in perfect competition are not produced
  • Consumers lose surplus on these units
  • Some producer surplus is gained (transferred from consumers)
  • The net effect is a loss of total surplus = Deadweight Loss
DWL = ½ × (QPC − QM) × (PM − MC)
⚠️ Important Note: The DWL is not just a transfer from consumers to producers—it's a destruction of surplus that benefits no one! Similar to taxation: both monopoly and taxes create a gap between what buyers pay and what it costs to produce, both lead to reduced trade and DWL. Difference: Tax revenue goes to government; monopoly profit goes to private firm.

Key Insight: The deadweight loss from monopoly is the triangle between the demand curve, the MC curve, and the range of output from QM to QPC. This represents lost surplus from trades that would have occurred in perfect competition but don't happen under monopoly.

🔹 The Lerner Index (Measuring Market Power)

Definition: The Lerner Index measures the extent of a firm's market power by calculating the price-marginal cost margin as a percentage of price.

Lerner Index = (P − MC) / P
Lerner IndexMeaning
Higher Lerner IndexMore market power
Lower Lerner IndexLess market power
Example: If P = 11 and MC = 2:
  • Lerner Index = (11 − 2) / 11 = 9/11 ≈ 0.82
  • Interpretation: 82% of the price represents a markup over marginal cost
  • The firm has substantial market power
Lerner Index = 1 / |ε|
If...Then...
Demand is inelastic (|ε| is small)Lerner Index is high → More market power
Demand is elastic (|ε| is large)Lerner Index is low → Less market power
⚠️ Key Insight: A monopolist will always operate where demand is elastic (|ε| > 1) because that's where MR is positive and the positive effect of the price reduction outweighs the negative effect.

🔹 Price Discrimination

Definition: Price discrimination is the practice of selling the same good at different prices to different customers, even when production costs are the same.

Conditions for Price Discrimination

  • Market Power: The firm must have some control over price
  • Different Consumer Groups: Consumers must have different willingness to pay
  • No Arbitrage: Consumers who buy at a low price can't resell to those paying a high price

Three Types of Price Discrimination

1. First-Degree (Perfect) Price Discrimination

  • What it is: Charging each customer exactly their maximum willingness to pay
  • Result: The monopolist captures ALL consumer surplus
  • Efficiency: Output reaches the competitive level → No deadweight loss!
  • Real-world: Rare in practice, but Big Data/Amazon might approximate it
⚠️ Key Insight: The producer has an incentive to sell to every consumer as long as their WTP > MC

2. Second-Degree Price Discrimination

  • What it is: Price varies with quantity purchased or quality level
  • Key Feature: Consumers reveal their WTP through their choices (self-revelation)
  • Examples: Quantity discounts (buy 2, get 1 free); Business class vs. economy class; Train tickets (peak vs. off-peak); All-you-can-eat vs. à la carte

3. Third-Degree Price Discrimination

  • What it is: Charging different prices to different identifiable groups
  • Key Feature: Statistical discrimination based on observable characteristics
  • Examples: Student discounts, Senior citizen discounts, Geographic pricing, Gender-based pricing
  • Rule: Groups with less elastic demand get charged higher prices
Profit maximization requires MR1 = MR2 = MC

Welfare Effects of Price Discrimination

  • ✅ Can increase welfare: Some consumers who couldn't afford the good at the single monopoly price can now purchase it
  • ❌ Transfers surplus: From consumers to producers (especially in first-degree)
  • ⚖️ Overall effect: Can reduce or eliminate deadweight loss in some cases!
Real-World Example: Pharmaceutical companies charging lower prices for HIV drugs in poor countries (while charging high prices in rich countries) increases access and saves lives! Note: Parallel imports must be prohibited to make this work.

🔹 Natural Monopoly Regulation

The Regulatory Trade-off: Natural monopolies create a dilemma: it's more efficient to have one firm produce, but that firm will exploit its market power.

Option 1: Marginal Cost Pricing (First Best)

Set P = MC

  • ✅ Eliminates deadweight loss → Efficient!
  • ❌ Firm makes losses (because MC < AC)
  • → Requires subsidies or nationalization

Option 2: Average Cost Pricing (Second Best)

Set P = AC

  • ✅ Firm breaks even (no losses)
  • ❌ Some deadweight loss remains
  • ❌ Some consumers are excluded

Option 3: Administered Pricing

Set price between MC and AC (MC < P < AC)

  • ✅ Smaller losses than marginal cost pricing
  • ❌ Still requires compensation

Real-World Solutions

  • Nationalization: Government runs the natural monopoly (e.g., SBB/CFF/FFS, postal service)
  • Subsidies: Government compensates the firm for losses
  • Do Nothing: If the market failure is deemed small compared to imperfections of public policies
Nobel Prize Connection: Jean Tirole (2014 Nobel Prize) studied the economics of regulation, including these very issues. Jean-Jacques Laffont was also a pioneer in this field.

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Profit Maximization

Scenario: Q = 12 − P, FC = 16, VC = 2Q. Find profit-maximizing quantity.

Solution: TR = 12Q − Q², MR = 12 − 2Q, MC = 2. Set MR = MC → Q = 5. P = 7. π = 35 − 26 = 9.

Scenario 2: Comparing to Perfect Competition

Scenario: Q = 12 − P, TC = 16 + 2Q. Compare monopoly to competition.

Solution: Monopoly: Q = 5, P = 7. Competition: P = MC = 2, Q = 10. DWL = ½ × (10 − 5) × (7 − 2) = 12.5.

Scenario 3: Price Discrimination

Scenario: Students (100): WTP = 8; Professors (100): WTP = 16; MC = 4. Which pricing strategy maximizes profit?

Solution: Uniform P=16 → Profit = 1,200. Uniform P=8 → Profit = 800. Price discrimination → Profit = 1,600. Price discrimination maximizes profit.

Scenario 4: Lerner Index

Scenario: Q = 200 − 10P, TC = 100 + 0.8Q + Q²/100. Find optimal quantity, price, and Lerner Index.

Solution: P = 20 − 0.1Q, MR = 20 − 0.2Q, MC = 0.8 + 0.02Q. Q = 87.27, P = 11.27. MC = 2.55. Lerner Index = (11.27 − 2.55)/11.27 = 0.77.

Scenario 5: Natural Monopoly Regulation

Scenario: P = 10 − Q, TC = 10 + 2Q. Compare unregulated, marginal cost pricing, and average cost pricing.

Solution: Unregulated: Q=4, P=6, Profit=6. Marginal Cost Pricing: Q=8, P=2, Loss=10. Average Cost Pricing: Q≈6.45, P≈3.55, Profit=0.

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing MR and Price: "A monopolist maximizes profit where P = MC." FALSE. The rule is MR = MC, NOT P = MC.
  • Thinking Monopolist Can Charge Any Price: FALSE. The monopolist can choose the price, but it must accept the quantity that consumers demand at that price.
  • Forgetting the Price Cut Affects ALL Units: "MR = P because the firm earns P on the additional unit." FALSE. The firm must lower the price on all units sold.
  • Thinking All Monopolies Are Bad: FALSE. Natural monopolies can be efficient, price discrimination can reduce deadweight loss, and patents encourage innovation.
  • Confusing Price Discrimination Types: Student discounts are third-degree (based on observable characteristics), not second-degree.

📝 Practice Questions

Q1: A monopolist maximizes profit where:
✅ Correct Answer: B
All firms maximize profit where MR = MC. In monopoly, since MR < P, this means P > MC.
Q2: For a monopolist, MR is less than P because:
✅ Correct Answer: B
To sell more output, a monopolist must lower the price on ALL units sold, not just the additional unit. This is why MR < P.
Q3: The Lerner Index measures:
✅ Correct Answer: B
The Lerner Index = (P − MC)/P = 1/|ε|. It measures the markup over marginal cost as a percentage of price.
Q4: Student discounts are an example of:
✅ Correct Answer: C
Student discounts are third-degree price discrimination because they're based on observable group characteristics (student status).
Q5: In a natural monopoly, breaking up the firm into multiple competitors would:
✅ Correct Answer: B
In a natural monopoly, one firm can produce at lower cost than multiple firms due to economies of scale. Breaking up the firm would increase average costs.
