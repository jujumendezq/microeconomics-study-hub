⚙️ 6. Technology & Cost of Production

Production functions, returns to scale, and the economics of innovation

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Define a firm according to the classical (technology) approach
  • Explain what a production function represents and how it summarizes technology
  • Distinguish between short-term and long-term production decisions
  • Explain the Law of Diminishing Marginal Product and why it matters
  • Calculate and classify Returns to Scale (Constant, Increasing, Decreasing)
  • Identify efficient vs. inefficient production points on a graph
  • Analyze the impact of innovation/technological progress on production
  • Define and differentiate between: Total Cost (TC), Fixed Cost (FC), Variable Cost (VC), Average Cost (AC), Average Variable Cost (AVC), and Marginal Cost (MC)
  • Explain the relationship between technology and cost functions
  • Understand how costs depend on the time horizon

🔹 Topic Introduction

This chapter is about how firms turn inputs (labor, capital, machinery) into outputs (goods and services). Think of it as the "recipe book" for a business. Understanding production functions is crucial because it explains:

  • Why companies can't just keep hiring more workers forever and expect the same growth
  • Why some industries are dominated by a few large firms (like Boeing or Facebook)
  • Why innovation is the engine of long-term economic growth
  • How businesses decide how much to produce and at what cost
  • How different types of costs (fixed, variable, average, marginal) shape firm decisions
Why should you care? Whether you're running a lemonade stand or analyzing a multinational corporation, production functions tell you the fundamental limits and possibilities of turning resources into value. This knowledge helps explain real-world phenomena like why the Soviet Union's growth eventually stalled, why Apple keeps innovating, why your favorite products get cheaper and better over time, and why social media companies can expand globally with minimal extra cost.

Three Approaches to Defining a Firm

The Technology Approach (Focus of this Chapter)

Simple Version: A firm is a "black box" that takes inputs (factors of production) and transforms them into outputs (goods and services).

The Bakery Example:
  • Inputs: Flour, eggs, sugar (natural resources), ovens (capital), bakers (labor), kitchen (land)
  • Technology (Recipe): The specific method of combining inputs
  • Output: Cakes

Why This Matters: The technology approach helps us understand production possibilities, focuses on efficiency—getting the most output from given inputs, and forms the foundation for understanding costs and supply decisions.

🔹 The Production Function

Definition: The production function describes the maximum output a firm can produce given a specific set of inputs and the available technology.

Simple Version: It's the "recipe" that shows how many units of output (Q) you get from using certain amounts of labor (L), capital (K), land (T), energy (E), and natural resources (M).

q = f(K, L, T, E, M)
VariableDefinition
qQuantity of output
KPhysical capital (machines, buildings, equipment)
LLabor (number of workers, hours worked)
TLand
EEnergy
MNatural resources

Simplification: In this course, we often focus on the two main factors:

q = f(K, L)
  • K = Capital (fixed in the short run, variable in the long run)
  • L = Labor (typically variable in the short run)

The "Black Box" Concept

  • The function f is a "black box"—we don't need to know exactly how inputs transform into outputs
  • It summarizes the best possible way of combining inputs to produce output
  • It represents the technological frontier—the maximum output possible
Real-World Example: Making a Cake
  • Labor: Time spent preparing the cake
  • Capital: Blender and other utensils
  • Land: Your kitchen
  • Natural Resources: Flour, eggs, ingredients
  • Intermediates: Edible decorations
  • Technology (Recipe): The method of combining ingredients

🔹 Short-Term vs. Long-Term Production

FeatureShort-TermLong-Term
DefinitionA period where at least one input is fixedA period where all inputs can be adjusted
Typical Fixed InputCapital (K) - factories, equipmentNone - everything is variable
Typical Variable InputLabor (L) - can hire/fire workersAll inputs can change
Nature of DecisionsDay-to-day operational decisionsStrategic planning decisions
IrreversibilityLong-term decisions are irreversible in short termDecisions can be fully implemented
⚠️ Important Caveat: Most of the time, we assume L is flexible and K is fixed in the short run. However, in some cases, it's the other way around! Example: Countries where firing workers is almost impossible (e.g., with strong labor protections). In this case, labor is fixed in the short run, while capital might be adjustable.

Why This Distinction Matters:

  • In the short run, firms face constraints—they can't instantly build new factories
  • In the long run, firms can redesign their entire operation
  • This distinction explains why marginal product eventually diminishes
  • Cost functions differ between short and long run

🔹 Marginal vs. Average Product

Marginal Product of Labor (MPL)

Definition: The additional output produced by one additional unit of labor.

MPL = Δq / ΔL

Example: If hiring one more worker increases output from 100 to 108 units, the MPL = 8 units.

Marginal Revenue (Value) of Labor

Definition: The additional revenue generated by one additional unit of labor.

Marginal Revenue of Labor = p × MPL

where p = selling price per unit

Average Product of Labor (APL)

Definition: How many units each worker produces on average.

APL = q / L

Example: If 5 workers produce 100 units, APL = 100/5 = 20 units per worker.

Average Revenue (Value) per Worker

Average Revenue per Worker = p × q / L
ConceptFormulaWhat It Tells Us
Marginal ProductΔq/ΔLOutput of the last worker
Average Productq/LOutput of a typical worker
Marginal Revenuep × Δq/ΔLRevenue from the last worker
Average Revenuep × q/LRevenue from a typical worker
Key Insight: Laymen think about averages. Economists think at the margin.

🔹 The Law of Diminishing Marginal Product

Definition: The Law of Diminishing Marginal Product states that as a firm increases one input (while holding others fixed), the additional output from each extra unit of that input will eventually decrease.

Simple Version: The more workers you add to a fixed kitchen, the less each new worker contributes to output.

Why This Happens

  • You have a fixed amount of capital (machines, space, equipment)
  • Each new worker adds some output
  • But as more workers are crowded into the same space, they get in each other's way
  • Workers may have to share equipment, wait for machines, or bump into each other
  • Each additional worker therefore adds less than the previous worker
PropertyWhat It MeansMath Notation
Positive RelationshipMore labor → More outputdq/dL > 0
Diminishing ReturnsThe increase in output gets smallerd²q/dL² < 0
Concave ShapeCurve gets flatter as L increasesGraph is concave
⚠️ What If Marginal Product Becomes Negative? Theoretically possible: Too many workers get in each other's way and total output falls. Realistically: A rational firm stops hiring before this point. Graphically: The firm never operates on the downward-sloping portion of the production function.

🔹 Returns to Scale (Long-Term Production)

Definition: Returns to Scale describes what happens to output when all inputs are increased by the same proportion (e.g., doubling both Labor AND Capital).

TypeEffect on OutputMathematical ConditionReal-World Example
Constant Returns to Scale (CRS)Output doubles when inputs doubleΔq = 2Small bakery doubling both workers and ovens
Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS)Output more than doublesΔq > 2Boeing, Facebook, TikTok (network effects)
Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRS)Output less than doublesΔq < 2Farming (limited quality land), CEO talent

Why Do Returns to Scale Differ?

  • Increasing Returns (IRS): Specialization, network effects, indivisibilities, spreading fixed costs. Example: 52% of US private-sector employees work in firms with at least 500 employees.
  • Decreasing Returns (DRS): Limited quality of inputs, management difficulties, un-replicable factors. Example: Agriculture—the best land is used first, expansion uses poorer land.
  • Constant Returns (CRS): Proportional scaling. Example: A small consulting firm doubling employees and office space.

Variable Returns to Scale

Production functions can also exhibit variable returns to scale: IRS at small volumes (gains from specialization) and DRS at larger volumes (management difficulties). This creates the U-shaped average cost curve!

The Cobb-Douglas Shortcut
For a production function of the form: q = A × Kα × Lβ
Just sum the exponents (α + β):
  • α + β > 1 → Increasing Returns to Scale (IRS)
  • α + β = 1 → Constant Returns to Scale (CRS)
  • α + β < 1 → Decreasing Returns to Scale (DRS)

🔹 Technological Progress (Innovation)

Definition: Innovation/Technological Progress means:

  • Doing more with the same: Producing more output with the same amounts of inputs, OR
  • Doing the same with less: Producing the same output with smaller amounts of inputs
The Ultimate Growth Engine
The Problem: Without innovation, growth from accumulating one input eventually stops (diminishing returns).
The Solution: Innovation overcomes diminishing returns by making inputs more productive.

Moore's Law: Real-World Innovation Example

Gordon Moore's Law: The number of transistors squeezed into integrated circuits doubles approximately every year (revised to every two years).

  • Impact: Computers get exponentially more powerful, prices drop dramatically, same cost → massively more performance
  • This is innovation in action!

Historical Case Study: The Soviet Union (1928-1940)

  • The Phenomenon: Manufacturing output increased by more than 170%.
  • Three Main Ingredients: Big push in industry — heavy state-financed investment; Abundance of rural labor — moving workers from farms to cities; Collective farming — highly productive agricultural system
  • The Illusion of Growth: The Soviet Union appeared to grow faster than democracies. But growth came from accumulating more resources, not innovation. Marginal product of labor was close to zero in agriculture. Growth eventually stalled when diminishing returns hit.
⚠️ The Lesson: Growth achieved by accumulating more resources is not sustainable without innovation.

🔹 Production Costs

The Link Between Technology and Costs

  • The production function (technology) and cost function are two sides of the same coin
  • Technology determines physical relationships (inputs → outputs)
  • Costs determine monetary relationships (inputs → costs)
  • Better technology → lower costs → lower prices → higher supply
The Basic Principle: "Producing more costs more"
  • To produce more, you need more inputs
  • More inputs mean higher costs
  • But: The unit cost (cost per unit) can go UP OR DOWN depending on:
  • — Fixed costs being spread over more units
  • — Diminishing returns increasing costs
  • — Economies of scale

Fixed Costs (FC)

Definition: The portion of total costs that is invariant in the level of production (q).

  • Must be paid even if q = 0
  • Associated with setting up production facilities
  • Examples: Rent, equipment purchases, insurance, salaries of permanent staff
  • Formula: FC = TC when q = 0

Variable Costs (VC)

Definition: The portion of total costs that varies when the level of output (q) varies.

  • Changes with output level
  • Examples: Raw materials, hourly wages, electricity
  • Formula: VC = VC(q) — depends on q

Total Costs (TC)

TC(q) = FC + VC(q)

Average Cost (AC)

AC = TC / q

Average Variable Cost (AVC)

AVC = VC / q

Marginal Cost (MC)

MC = ΔTC / Δq
⚠️ Key Point: MC is the key concept for the firm's choice of output (as we'll see in the next chapter on supply).

🔹 The Shape of Cost Curves

The U-Shaped Average Cost (AC)

Why is AC "U-shaped"?

  • Declining portion: Fixed costs are "spread over" more units → AC falls
  • Rising portion: Diminishing returns → more inputs needed for each additional unit → AC rises

The U-Shaped Marginal Cost (MC)

  • Initial decline: Specialization and learning by doing → MC falls
  • Eventual rise: Diminishing returns → each extra unit costs more → MC rises

The Relationship Between AC and MC

📌 The Golden Rule:
  • When MC < AC, AC is falling
  • When MC > AC, AC is rising
  • When MC = AC, AC is at its minimum
Why? Think of MC as the "new score" and AC as the "average score." If the new score is below the average, the average falls. If the new score is above the average, the average rises.

Step-by-Step Example: Returns to Scale
q = K0.5 × L0.5. Initial: K = 36, L = 100 → q = 6 × 10 = 60.
Double inputs: K = 72, L = 200 → q = 8.49 × 14.14 ≈ 120.
Output doubled (60 → 120). α + β = 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 → Constant Returns to Scale.

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Returns to Scale

Scenario: q = K0.5 × L0.5. Calculate output when K = 100 and L = 64. If both inputs are doubled, what happens? What type of returns to scale?

Solution: q = 1000.5 × 640.5 = 10 × 8 = 80. Double inputs → q = 2000.5 × 1280.5 ≈ 14.14 × 11.31 ≈ 160. Output doubled → Constant Returns to Scale (α + β = 1).

Scenario 2: Social Media Returns to Scale

Scenario: Social media like TikTok can be easily expanded to more places without much additional cost. This is an example of:

Solution: Increasing Returns to Scale. Adding users (inputs) leads to more than proportional output growth because network effects mean each new user adds value beyond their own contribution.

Scenario 3: AC and MC Relationship

Scenario: Which statement is correct about AC and MC?

Solution: When AC = MC, the AC curve has a zero slope (it's at its minimum point).

Scenario 4: Innovation and Cost Reduction

Scenario: q = 10 × K0.5 × L0.5. K = 100, L = 64. What is current output? If innovation improves technology to A = 20, what happens?

Solution: q = 10 × 10 × 8 = 800. New q = 20 × 10 × 8 = 1,600. Output doubles with the same inputs. Cost curves shift downward (lower AC and MC at every q).

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Short-Run and Long-Run Concepts: Diminishing returns is a short-run concept (one input fixed). Returns to scale is about changing ALL inputs.
  • Confusing Marginal and Average: MC = cost of LAST unit, AC = average of ALL units.
  • Misinterpreting Shifts vs. Movements: Innovation SHIFTS the production function UP. Hiring more workers is a MOVEMENT ALONG the curve.
  • Getting Returns to Scale Backwards: Output doubles when inputs double = CONSTANT Returns. Output more than doubles = INCREASING Returns.
  • Forgetting Fixed Costs in Cost Calculations: Total cost at q=0 equals FIXED COSTS.

  • MC is U-shaped: falls then rises

Q2: If α + β = 1.3 in a Cobb-Douglas production function, what type of returns to scale does it exhibit?
✅ Correct Answer: B
α + β > 1 → Increasing Returns to Scale. Output grows more than proportionally to inputs.
Q3: When MC < AC, the AC curve is:
✅ Correct Answer: B
When MC < AC, the marginal cost is below the average, pulling the average down. So AC is falling.
Q4: Which of the following is a fixed cost?
✅ Correct Answer: C
Rent is a fixed cost because it doesn't change with the level of output. Raw materials, hourly wages, and electricity are variable costs.

✅ Correct Answer: B
Innovation shifts the production function UP (more output from same inputs) and shifts cost curves DOWN (lower costs at every output level).

📖 Glossary

Production Function
The relationship between inputs and the maximum output that can be produced given current technology.
Firm
An agent that produces goods/services; transforms inputs into outputs.
Diminishing Marginal Product
The tendency for the additional output from each extra unit of input to eventually decrease.
Short Run
A period in which at least one input is fixed.
Long Run
A period in which all inputs can be varied.
Returns to Scale
How output changes when ALL inputs are increased proportionally.
Innovation
An improvement that allows more output from the same inputs or the same output from fewer inputs.
Fixed Cost (FC)
Costs that must be paid regardless of output level.
Variable Cost (VC)
Costs that change with the level of output.
Total Cost (TC)
Sum of fixed and variable costs.
Average Cost (AC)
Total cost divided by quantity; cost per unit.
Average Variable Cost (AVC)
Variable cost divided by quantity.
Marginal Cost (MC)
Change in total cost from producing one additional unit.
Marginal Product of Labor (MPL)
Additional output from one more unit of labor.
Average Product of Labor (APL)
Total output divided by total labor.
Cobb-Douglas
A specific production function: q = A × Kα × Lβ.
Network Effects
When the value of a product increases as more people use it.
