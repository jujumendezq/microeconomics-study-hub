🏛️ 11. Public Goods

Non‑rival, non‑excludable · free‑riding, common resources

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Classify goods by their excludability and rivalry: private goods, public goods, common resources, and club goods
  • Explain the free‑rider problem and why it leads to underprovision of public goods
  • Analyze common resources and the tragedy of the commons
  • Identify real‑world examples: streetlights, overfishing, national defense, Elinor Ostrom's governance
  • Understand why government provision is often necessary for public goods
  • Evaluate policy solutions for common resources (quotas, taxes, property rights)

🔹 Topic Introduction

Welcome to the world of public goods! In the previous chapter, you learned about externalities – the spillover effects that create market failures. Public goods are a special type of market failure that arises from non‑excludability and non‑rivalry.

What's the problem with public goods?
Private markets often fail to provide public goods because people can enjoy the benefits without paying – this is called the free‑rider problem. National defense, street lighting, and basic research are examples of goods that the market would underprovide.

Why should you care? Public goods are everywhere:

  • National defense – you benefit even if you don't pay taxes
  • Street lights – you can use them without paying
  • Basic research – scientific discoveries benefit everyone
  • Clean air – everyone breathes it, but no one owns it

Understanding public goods helps you understand why governments fund certain things and why common resources like fisheries and forests are often overused.

🔹 Types of Goods

Goods can be classified along two dimensions:

ExcludableNon‑Excludable
RivalrousPrivate Goods (food, clothing, cars)Common Resources (fisheries, forests, water)
Non‑RivalrousClub Goods (cable TV, fire protection, toll roads)Public Goods (national defense, streetlights, knowledge)

1. Private Goods

  • Excludable? Yes – you can prevent others from using it
  • Rivalrous? Yes – one person's use reduces availability for others
  • Examples: Food, clothing, smartphones, housing
  • Market outcome: Efficiently provided by the market

2. Public Goods

  • Excludable? No – you can't prevent anyone from using it
  • Rivalrous? No – one person's use doesn't reduce availability for others
  • Examples: National defense, streetlights, air quality, basic research
  • Market outcome: Underprovided by the market (free‑rider problem)

3. Common Resources

  • Excludable? No – you can't prevent others from using it
  • Rivalrous? Yes – one person's use reduces availability for others
  • Examples: Fisheries, forests, groundwater, grazing land
  • Market outcome: Overused (tragedy of the commons)

4. Club Goods

  • Excludable? Yes – you can prevent others from using it
  • Rivalrous? No – one person's use doesn't reduce availability for others
  • Examples: Cable TV, fire protection, toll roads, private parks
  • Market outcome: Can be provided by the market

🔹 Public Goods

Definition: A public good is a good that is non‑excludable and non‑rivalrous.

  • Non‑excludable: You cannot prevent people from using the good, even if they don't pay for it.
  • Non‑rivalrous: One person's use of the good does not reduce its availability for others.
Example: Streetlight
Imagine a streetlight in your neighborhood. Everyone can benefit from it (non‑excludable), and one person's use doesn't reduce the light for others (non‑rivalrous). If the streetlight were privately provided, people would try to "free‑ride" – enjoy the benefit without paying. The result is underprovision – too few streetlights.

Free‑Rider Problem

The free‑rider problem occurs when people can enjoy the benefits of a good without paying for it. This leads to the market providing too little of the good.

The Streetlight Game:
  • Ten neighbors can each contribute $100 to install a streetlight worth $1,000.
  • If everyone contributes, the streetlight is installed and everyone benefits.
  • But each person thinks: "If I don't contribute, the others might still pay, and I'll still get the light for free."
  • Result: No one contributes, and the streetlight is not installed.

Social Value of Public Goods

Social Value = Sum of Individual Marginal Benefits
⚠️ Key Insight: For public goods, the social marginal benefit is the vertical sum of individual willingness to pay (WTP), not the horizontal sum (as with private goods). This is because the same unit of the good is consumed by everyone.

🔹 Common Resources

Definition: A common resource is a good that is non‑excludable but rivalrous.

  • Non‑excludable: You cannot prevent people from using the resource.
  • Rivalrous: One person's use reduces availability for others.

The Tragedy of the Commons

The tragedy of the commons occurs when individuals acting independently and rationally according to their own self‑interest behave contrary to the best interests of the whole group by overusing a shared resource.

Example: Overfishing
Imagine a shared fishing ground. Each fisherman catches as many fish as possible. No one thinks about preserving the stock for the future because the fish are a common resource – if they don't catch them, someone else will. The result is overfishing and the depletion of fish stocks.
Classic Example: Buffalo vs. Cows
Buffalo were a common resource in the American West – they were hunted to near extinction because no one owned them. Cows are a private good – owners have an incentive to maintain their herds. This illustrates the tragedy of the commons: commonly owned resources are often overused and depleted.

Elinor Ostrom's Governance

Elinor Ostrom (Nobel Prize 2009) showed that communities can successfully manage common resources without government regulation through:

  • Shared rules: Communities develop rules for resource use.
  • Monitoring: Communities monitor each other's behavior.
  • Sanctions: Communities impose penalties for rule violations.
  • Trust: Communities build trust through repeated interactions.
⚠️ Key Insight: Common resources are NOT the same as public goods. They are rivalrous, so they can be overused. The tragedy of the commons arises from non‑excludability combined with rivalry.

🔹 Real-World Examples

1. Streetlights

  • Type: Public good
  • Problem: Everyone benefits from streetlights, but no one wants to pay for them.
  • Solution: Government provision through taxes.

2. Overfishing

  • Type: Common resource
  • Problem: Shared fishing grounds are overfished because no one owns the fish.
  • Solution: Individual Transferable Quotas (ITQs), fishing quotas, or property rights.

3. Buffalo vs. Cows

  • Buffalo: Common resource → hunted to near extinction.
  • Cows: Private good → owners maintain herds.
  • Lesson: Property rights prevent the tragedy of the commons.

4. National Defense

  • Type: Public good
  • Problem: Everyone benefits from national defense, but no one can be excluded.
  • Solution: Government provision through taxes.

5. Club Goods

  • Type: Club good (excludable, non‑rivalrous)
  • Examples: Cable TV, fire protection, toll roads, private parks.
  • Market outcome: Can be provided by the market through membership or subscription.

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Classification of Goods

Scenario: Classify the following goods: a) National defense, b) A fishing ground, c) Cable TV, d) A sandwich.

Solution:

  • National defense: Public good (non‑excludable, non‑rivalrous)
  • Fishing ground: Common resource (non‑excludable, rivalrous)
  • Cable TV: Club good (excludable, non‑rivalrous)
  • Sandwich: Private good (excludable, rivalrous)

Scenario 2: Free-Rider Problem

Scenario: A neighborhood of 10 residents wants to install a security camera worth $1,000. Each resident values the camera at $150. Will the camera be installed?

Solution: Total value = 10 × 150 = $1,500 > $1,000. The camera is socially desirable. But each resident thinks: "If I don't contribute, the others might still pay." The free‑rider problem leads to underprovision – the camera may not be installed without government intervention.

Scenario 3: Tragedy of the Commons

Scenario: A shared grazing land can support 100 cows. Each cow yields $200 of profit. If there are 150 cows, each cow yields $100 of profit. How many cows will be grazed?

Solution: Each farmer adds a cow until marginal profit = 0. With 100 cows, profit/cow = $200. With 150 cows, profit/cow = $100. The tragedy of the commons leads to overgrazing – more cows than the land can sustainably support.

Scenario 4: Elinor Ostrom's Governance

Scenario: A fishing community has used a common fishing ground for generations. What rules might they develop to prevent overfishing?

Solution: Communities can develop rules for resource use, monitor each other, impose sanctions, and build trust through repeated interactions. This is the basis of Elinor Ostrom's Nobel Prize work.

Scenario 5: Policy Solutions

Scenario: What policies can solve the overfishing problem?

Solution:

  • Individual Transferable Quotas (ITQs): Allocate fishing rights to individuals
  • Fishing quotas: Set limits on total catch
  • Property rights: Assign ownership of fishing grounds
  • Taxes: Tax fishing to reduce overuse
  • Regulation: Limit fishing seasons, gear types, or areas

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Public Goods with Common Resources: Public goods are non‑rivalrous and non‑excludable (underprovided). Common resources are rivalrous and non‑excludable (overused).
  • Confusing Public Goods with Club Goods: Public goods are non‑excludable; club goods are excludable.
  • Thinking Free‑Rider Problem Only Applies to Public Goods: Free‑riding also occurs with common resources (e.g., overfishing).
  • Forgetting Elinor Ostrom's Work: Communities can manage common resources without government intervention.
