🎯 12. Uncertainty

Risk, expected utility, insurance, and diversification

📌 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what a random variable is and calculate its expected value
  • Characterize different attitudes towards risk (risk-averse, risk-neutral, risk-loving)
  • Identify how the shape of a utility function reveals risk preferences
  • Explain why risk-averse people are willing to pay a risk premium
  • Understand how insurance and diversification can reduce risk

🔹 Topic Introduction

Life is full of uncertainty. Every day, we make decisions without knowing exactly what will happen tomorrow. Should you buy that house? Will it increase or decrease in value? Should you invest in stocks or keep your money in a savings account? Will it rain on the day of your outdoor concert? This chapter introduces you to how economists think about decision-making under uncertainty.

Why should you care? Understanding this material is crucial because it forms the foundation for finance courses you'll take next semester, and it explains why most people buy insurance, why diversification is important in investing, and why people often make seemingly irrational decisions (like buying lottery tickets despite terrible odds).

🔹 Random Variables

Definition: A random variable is simply something whose future value we don't know for certain. It could be the outcome of a coin toss (heads or tails), the value of your house next year, or the profit from a concert that depends on the weather.

Real-World Example: When you buy a house today, you don't know what it will be worth in 5 years. Will the neighborhood improve? Will a new highway be built nearby? Will there be a fire? The future value of your house is a random variable.

Why do we need to understand random variables? Many economic decisions involve uncertain outcomes. If we knew the future with certainty, decision-making would be simple—we'd just choose the best option. But because we don't know the future, we need tools to help us make better decisions despite uncertainty.

Key Elements

  • Discrete random variables: Take on specific, countable values (like heads or tails)
  • Continuous random variables: Can take on any value within a range (like the exact temperature tomorrow)
  • Each possible outcome has a probability associated with it

🔹 Probability

Definition: Probability is the frequency with which something occurs. It's a number between 0 and 1 that tells us how likely an event is to happen.

Real-World Example:
  • If you flip a fair coin, the probability of getting heads is 0.5 (or 1/2).
  • If you roll a fair six-sided die, the probability of rolling a 4 is 1/6.

Why do probabilities matter? Probabilities quantify uncertainty. Without probabilities, we'd have no way to compare different uncertain outcomes. Probabilities allow us to calculate expected values and make rational decisions.

Key Elements

  • Probabilities must sum to 1 for all possible outcomes
  • P(heads) = 0.5 and P(tails) = 0.5 → 0.5 + 0.5 = 1
  • For a house: P(fire) = 0.01, P(no fire) = 0.99 → 0.01 + 0.99 = 1
  • A "fair" coin means heads and tails are equally likely (both 0.5)

🔹 Expected Value (EV)

Definition: The expected value is a weighted average of all possible outcomes, where the weights are the probabilities of each outcome occurring. It's what you would get "on average" if the situation were repeated many, many times.

EV = Σ Pi × Xi
Real-World Example: In the concert example, the expected profit is (0.5 × 15,000) + (0.5 × -5,000) = 5,000 CHF. If you organized this concert every night for 1,000 nights, you'd expect to make about 5,000 CHF per night on average.

Why is expected value important? Expected value gives us a single number that summarizes all possible outcomes. It's a benchmark for decision-making. However, it's important to understand that the expected value is rarely what actually happens—the house worth 79,600 CHF on average will actually be worth either 80,000 CHF or 40,000 CHF.

⚠️ Key Insight: EV is a theoretical benchmark, not an actual outcome. EV is what you'd observe on average after infinite repetitions. EV doesn't capture risk—two gambles can have the same EV but very different risk profiles.

🔹 Fair Lottery

Definition: A fair lottery is a gamble where the expected value is zero. You have an equal chance of winning and losing the same amount.

Real-World Example: I flip a coin. If it's heads, you pay me 10 CHF. If it's tails, I pay you 10 CHF. EV = (0.5 × 10) + (0.5 × -10) = 0.

Why are fair lotteries useful? Fair lotteries are useful for identifying risk preferences:

  • Risk-averse: Rejects a fair lottery → prefers certainty
  • Risk-loving: Accepts a fair lottery → enjoys the gamble
  • Risk-neutral: Indifferent → only cares about expected value

🔹 Attitudes Towards Risk

People have different attitudes towards risk. These attitudes determine whether they'll accept a gamble or prefer a certain outcome.

🔹 Expected Utility (EU)

Definition: Expected utility is the weighted average of the utilities from each possible outcome, where the weights are the probabilities of each outcome occurring.

EU = Σ Pi × U(Xi)
Real-World Example: In the concert example, if U(ω) = √ω:
  • Utility with rain (25,000 CHF) = √25,000 ≈ 158
  • Utility with sun (45,000 CHF) = √45,000 ≈ 212
  • Expected Utility = 0.5 × 158 + 0.5 × 212 = 185

Why is expected utility important? Expected utility is what matters for decision-making under uncertainty. People don't maximize expected wealth—they maximize expected utility. This explains why risk-averse people reject fair lotteries even when the expected value is zero.

⚠️ Key Insight: For risk-averse people: EU < U(EV). For risk-loving people: EU > U(EV). For risk-neutral people: EU = U(EV).

Key Insight: For a risk-averse person, U(EV) > EU. The difference between EV and the certainty equivalent is the risk premium – the maximum amount they'd pay to avoid risk.

🔹 Risk Premium

Definition: The risk premium is the maximum amount a risk-averse person is willing to pay to avoid risk. It's the difference between the expected value of a gamble and the certainty equivalent (the amount that gives the same utility as the gamble).

Risk Premium = EV − Certainty Equivalent
Real-World Example: In the concert example, you might be willing to pay 10 CHF for insurance that guarantees you'll have 35,000 CHF regardless of the weather. Even if the insurance company charged you 11 CHF (an extra 1 CHF), you might still buy it because you value certainty. The risk premium is the extra amount you're willing to pay above the fair insurance premium.
⚠️ Key Insight: The more concave the utility function (the more diminishing marginal utility), the larger the risk premium. Risk-averse people pay to eliminate uncertainty.

🔹 Insurance

Definition: Insurance is a contract where you pay a premium in exchange for protection against a specific risk. If the bad event happens, the insurance company pays you a benefit.

Real-World Example: You pay 500 CHF per year for car insurance. If you get into an accident, the insurance company pays for repairs. You're transferring the risk from yourself to the insurance company.
TypeDefinitionExample
Actuarial (Fair) InsurancePremium = expected lossIf chance of accident = 5% and damage = 10,000 CHF → fair premium = 500 CHF
Risk PremiumAdditional amount above fair premiumRisk-averse person pays 550 CHF even though fair premium is 500 CHF
Real InsuranceCompanies charge more than fair premium to cover costs and make profitInsurance premium = 600 CHF (includes administration costs)
Actuarial (Fair) Premium = P(bad event) × Loss amount
Example: Health Insurance
  • A household has 30,000 CHF income
  • There's a 0.5 probability someone gets sick and incurs 10,000 CHF in medical expenses
  • Fair premium = 0.5 × 10,000 = 5,000 CHF
  • Without insurance: EU = 0.5 × U(30,000) + 0.5 × U(20,000) = 1,000
  • With fair insurance: Income = 25,000 CHF (regardless of illness) → Utility = 1,025
  • Since 1,025 > 1,000, the household is better off with insurance
⚠️ Key Insight: Risk-averse people will buy insurance even if it's "unfair" (premium > expected loss) because they value the elimination of risk.

🔹 Diversification

Definition: Diversification is a strategy to reduce risk by spreading investments across multiple assets or activities that are not perfectly correlated with each other.

Real-World Example: Don't put all your eggs in one basket. Instead of investing all your money in one company's stock, invest in stocks from different industries. If one industry declines, others may do well.
Example: Sunglasses & Umbrellas
  • Sunglasses revenue: Sunny = 30,000 CHF, Rainy = 12,000 CHF
  • Umbrellas revenue: Sunny = 12,000 CHF, Rainy = 30,000 CHF
  • Each weather has 0.5 probability
  • Only sunglasses: EV = 0.5 × 30,000 + 0.5 × 12,000 = 21,000 CHF (risky)
  • Only umbrellas: EV = 0.5 × 12,000 + 0.5 × 30,000 = 21,000 CHF (risky)
  • Both (diversified): EV = 21,000 CHF regardless of weather! Risk eliminated
⚠️ Key Insight: Diversification works because different risks are often not perfectly correlated. When one investment performs poorly, another might perform well, smoothing out your overall returns. However, systematic risk (like a global recession) can't be diversified away.

🧮 Key Formulas

EV = Σ Pi × Xi (Expected Value)
EU = Σ Pi × U(Xi) (Expected Utility)
Actuarial (Fair) Premium = P(bad event) × Loss amount
Risk Premium = EV − Certainty Equivalent
Diversification: Combine uncorrelated risky assets to reduce risk

📝 Exam Scenarios & Problem-Solving

Scenario 1: Expected Value Calculation

Scenario: A gamble offers a 0.3 probability of winning 100 CHF and a 0.7 probability of winning 20 CHF. What is the expected value?

Solution: EV = 0.3 × 100 + 0.7 × 20 = 30 + 14 = 44 CHF

Scenario 2: Expected Utility and Risk Aversion

Scenario: U(ω) = √ω. Wealth = 100 CHF. Offered gamble: 0.5 chance to win 21 CHF (wealth = 121), 0.5 chance to lose 19 CHF (wealth = 81). Calculate EU and U(EV). Is this person risk-averse?

Solution:

  • EV = 0.5 × 121 + 0.5 × 81 = 101
  • U(121) = √121 = 11, U(81) = √81 = 9
  • EU = 0.5 × 11 + 0.5 × 9 = 10
  • U(EV) = U(101) = √101 ≈ 10.05
  • Since 10 < 10.05, EU < U(EV) → Risk-averse

Scenario 3: Fair Insurance Premium

Scenario: What is the fair premium for insurance against a 20,000 CHF loss that occurs with 0.1 probability?

Solution: Fair Premium = 0.1 × 20,000 = 2,000 CHF

Scenario 4: Diversification

Scenario: You can invest all your money in an asset that returns 10% or 0% with equal probability, or you can split your investment between two unrelated assets with the same returns. Which option reduces risk?

Solution: Splitting between two unrelated assets reduces risk because when one performs poorly, the other might perform well. This is diversification.

Scenario 5: Open-Air Concert

Scenario: You organize open-air concerts. You can hire a local band or a world-famous band. Both have EV = 5,000 CHF. Local band: Rain → -5,000 CHF, Sun → 15,000 CHF. Famous band: Rain → -150,000 CHF, Sun → 160,000 CHF. Which do you choose?

Solution:

  • Risk-averse: Choose Local Band (avoid large loss)
  • Risk-loving: Choose Famous Band (chance of huge profit)
  • Risk-neutral: Indifferent (same EV)
Answer: Most people choose the local band because they're risk-averse. The expected values are the same, but the local band has much lower downside risk.

Common Exam Traps

  • Confusing Expected Value with Expected Utility: EV is weighted average of monetary outcomes; EU is weighted average of utilities. Risk-averse people maximize EU, not EV.
  • Confusing Risk-Aversion with Risk-Loving: Risk-Averse → Concave → EU < U(EV). Risk-Loving → Convex → EU > U(EV).
  • Thinking Insurance Premiums Are Always Fair: Fair premium = expected loss. Real insurance premiums include a risk premium (profit, administrative costs).
  • Forgetting Diversification Requires Uncorrelated Events: Diversification reduces risk when risks are not perfectly correlated.
  • Believing Expected Value Happens in Reality: EV is a theoretical average, not what actually happens.

📖 Glossary

Random Variable
A variable whose future value is uncertain.
Probability
The frequency with which something occurs; a number between 0 and 1.
Expected Value (EV)
The weighted average of all possible outcomes.
Fair Lottery
A gamble with an expected value of zero.
Risk-Averse
Prefers certainty over a gamble with the same EV; concave utility function.
Risk-Loving
Prefers a gamble over certainty with the same EV; convex utility function.
Risk-Neutral
Indifferent between certainty and a gamble with the same EV; linear utility.
Utility Function of Wealth
A mathematical relationship mapping wealth to satisfaction.
Expected Utility (EU)
The weighted average of utilities from all possible outcomes.
Risk Premium
The maximum amount a risk-averse person will pay to eliminate risk.
Actuarial (Fair) Insurance
Insurance with premium exactly equal to the expected loss.
Certainty Equivalent
The amount of money with certainty that gives the same utility as a gamble.
Diversification
A strategy to reduce risk by combining uncorrelated assets.
Systematic Risk
Risk that cannot be diversified away (affects all investments).
Idiosyncratic Risk
Risk specific to an individual investment that can be diversified away.
Diminishing Marginal Utility
The decrease in additional satisfaction from each additional unit of wealth.
